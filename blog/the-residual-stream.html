Arc 4: Transformers from First Principles

The Residual Stream: A Mental Model for Transformers

16 min read

A single vector flows through the whole network. Each block reads from it, computes, and adds its result back.

So what is a transformer, really?

Not "attention over token sequences" or "the architecture from Vaswani et al." I mean: what's the right mental picture to carry around when you're trying to reason about why a specific layer does what it does, or why a 70-billion-parameter model can be decomposed into meaningful circuits?

Most introductions describe a transformer as a stack of layers that processes data top-to-bottom. Input goes in at the bottom, gets transformed layer by layer, and a prediction comes out at the top. That isn't wrong, but it misses the single most clarifying insight about the architecture.

Here's the picture. A transformer is a residual stream. There's a single vector (one per token position) that flows through the entire network. Attention blocks and feed-forward blocks don't transform this vector. They read from it, compute something, and add their result back. The stream accumulates information. The blocks just contribute to it.

This framing comes from Elhage et al. at Anthropic, and it's, I think, the most useful thing I've internalized about the transformer architecture. The rest of this post builds up to it.

Where skip connections come from

To understand the residual stream, we need to start in 2015, with a problem that had nothing to do with language.

Computer vision researchers wanted to make convolutional networks deeper. More layers should mean more capacity, better features, better performance. But in practice, once you stacked too many layers, something broke. The network got worse, and not because of overfitting. Training loss itself got worse. A 56-layer CNN performed worse than a 20-layer CNN on the training set.

Kaiming He and collaborators at Microsoft Research figured out why and published a fix that changed deep learning permanently. The problem was vanishing gradients (we covered those in Post 1.5). When you have a long chain of matrix multiplications and nonlinearities, the gradient signal that backprop relies on gets exponentially squashed as it travels backward. By the time gradients reach the early layers, they're essentially zero, and the early layers stop learning.

The fix was simple. Instead of asking each layer to compute the output directly, ask it to compute a correction to the input, and then add that correction to the original input:

xout=x+F(x)\mathbf{x}_{\text{out}} = \mathbf{x} + F(\mathbf{x})xout​=x+F(x)

FFF is whatever the layer computes (convolutions, nonlinearities, normalization). The key is that x\mathbf{x}x passes through unchanged via the identity path. The layer only has to learn F(x)F(\mathbf{x})F(x), the residual between the desired output and the input. If a layer has nothing useful to contribute, FFF can learn to output zero and the input passes through unharmed.

That's a skip connection. It solves the gradient problem because the identity path gives gradients a highway that bypasses all the nonlinear computation. During backpropagation, the gradient can flow directly from layer nnn to layer 0 without being multiplied by any weight matrix.

Deep stack: with and without skip connections

A signal enters at the top of an 8-layer stack. On the left, each layer replaces its input (x ← F(x)). By the bottom, the signal barely resembles the input. On the right, each layer adds its output to the stream (x ← x + F(x)). The original signal persists all the way down.

The left side of that picture is the pre-2015 regime. The right side is what made very deep networks trainable in the first place, and what every transformer quietly uses today. He et al. called their networks Residual Networks (ResNets), and they immediately blew past every depth record in image classification. Their 152-layer network won ImageNet 2015.

But I think the deeper lesson wasn't about depth. It was about what the identity path means architecturally. Veit et al. showed in 2016 that ResNets behave like ensembles of many shallow networks. When you have skip connections, the data doesn't take a single serial path through all layers. It takes exponentially many paths of varying lengths, because at each layer it can either go through FFF or skip it via the identity. The network is less like a deep pipeline and more like a collection of parallel contributions, all added together.

Keep that picture in your head. That's the residual stream.

The transformer uses the same trick

Vaswani et al. built skip connections directly into the transformer architecture. Every transformer block has two sub-layers (a multi-head attention mechanism and a feed-forward network, sometimes called an MLP), and each sub-layer has a skip connection around it.

In the modern "pre-norm" variant (which is what GPT-2, LLaMA, and basically everything since 2020 uses), a single transformer block does this:

x←x+Attn(LN(x))\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} + \text{Attn}(\text{LN}(\mathbf{x}))x←x+Attn(LN(x)) x←x+FFN(LN(x))\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} + \text{FFN}(\text{LN}(\mathbf{x}))x←x+FFN(LN(x))

where LN\text{LN}LN is LayerNorm. Read these two lines carefully. The variable x\mathbf{x}x on the left side is being updated by adding something to its previous value. It's not being replaced.

That means x\mathbf{x}x is a running sum. It starts as a token embedding. Each attention block reads from it, computes an update, and adds the update back. Each FFN block does the same. After NNN layers, the final value of x\mathbf{x}x is:

xfinal=xembed+∑l=1N[Attnl(LN(x(l)))+FFNl(LN(x(l)))]\mathbf{x}_{\text{final}} = \mathbf{x}_{\text{embed}} + \sum_{l=1}^{N} \Big[\text{Attn}_l(\text{LN}(\mathbf{x}^{(l)})) + \text{FFN}_l(\text{LN}(\mathbf{x}^{(l)}))\Big]xfinal​=xembed​+l=1∑N​[Attnl​(LN(x(l)))+FFNl​(LN(x(l)))]

The token embedding is still in there. It was never overwritten. Every layer's contribution is still in there, summed together. That sum is the residual stream.

The thing I want you to notice is the additive structure. The stream at the end is the embedding plus six additive corrections. Nothing was overwritten. Nothing was replaced. The attention block in Layer 2 didn't "process the output of Layer 1." It read from the stream (which contains the contributions of everything before it), computed an update, and wrote that update back. The distinction matters.

Why this framing matters

Once you see the transformer as a residual stream, several things click into place.

Every component reads from the same bus and writes back to it. An attention head in layer 5 can, in principle, read information that was written by an attention head in layer 1 directly through the residual stream. It doesn't have to go "through" layers 2, 3, and 4 in any meaningful sense. Those layers made their own additive contributions, sure, but the information from layer 1 persists in the sum.

Information accumulates rather than being transformed. In a standard feedforward network without skip connections, each layer replaces its input with something new. The representation at layer 10 bears little resemblance to the representation at layer 1. In a transformer, the representation at layer 10 contains the representation at layer 1, plus corrections from layers 2 through 10. Early features persist. This is why you can often "read off" simple features (token identity, position, part of speech) from any layer, not just the layer that computed them.

The stream is a communication channel. This is the key insight from Elhage et al. If every block reads from the stream and writes to the stream, then the stream is how blocks communicate with each other. An attention head in layer 3 can write a specific pattern into the residual stream in dimensions that an FFN block in layer 6 has learned to read. They communicate through the shared vector, not through any direct connection. That's what interpretability researchers mean when they talk about "transformer circuits": compositions of readers and writers that communicate through the residual stream.

The stream as a communication channel
1. baseline stream — just the embedding

Attn 1 writes a feature into two specific dimensions of the stream. Layers 2 and 3 add their own corrections in other dimensions. By the time FFN 4 reads, the original feature is still sitting there in dims 1 and 4, untouched. The middle layers neither relayed the message nor blocked it.

Layer deletions don't kill the model. If you surgically remove a middle layer from a transformer, performance degrades, but the model doesn't collapse entirely. That makes sense under the residual stream framing. You removed one additive contribution. The rest of the stream is still intact. In a model without skip connections, removing a middle layer would completely disconnect the layers above from the layers below.

The math, all in one place

Let me write out the full forward pass for a transformer decoder with NNN layers using pre-norm. This is what LLaMA, GPT-2 (approximately), Mistral, and most modern models do.

Given a sequence of token IDs t1,t2,…,tTt_1, t_2, \ldots, t_Tt1​,t2​,…,tT​:

1. Embedding. Look up each token ID in the embedding table EEE (as we built in Post 3.5) and add positional information:

xi(0)=E[ti]+PE(i)\mathbf{x}_i^{(0)} = E[t_i] + \text{PE}(i)xi(0)​=E[ti​]+PE(i)

2. Transformer blocks. For each layer l=1,…,Nl = 1, \ldots, Nl=1,…,N:

xi(l)=xi(l−1)+Attnl ⁣(LN1(l)(x1(l−1)),…,LN1(l)(xT(l−1)))i\mathbf{x}_i^{(l)} = \mathbf{x}_i^{(l-1)} + \text{Attn}_l\!\left(\text{LN}_1^{(l)}(\mathbf{x}_1^{(l-1)}), \ldots, \text{LN}_1^{(l)}(\mathbf{x}_T^{(l-1)})\right)_ixi(l)​=xi(l−1)​+Attnl​(LN1(l)​(x1(l−1)​),…,LN1(l)​(xT(l−1)​))i​ xi(l)←xi(l)+FFNl ⁣(LN2(l)(xi(l)))\mathbf{x}_i^{(l)} \leftarrow \mathbf{x}_i^{(l)} + \text{FFN}_l\!\left(\text{LN}_2^{(l)}(\mathbf{x}_i^{(l)})\right)xi(l)​←xi(l)​+FFNl​(LN2(l)​(xi(l)​))

I wrote the attention step to make clear that it operates over all positions at once (it computes interactions between tokens), while the FFN step operates on each position independently.

3. Output. Apply a final layer norm and project to vocabulary logits:

logitsi=LNfinal(xi(N))⋅U\text{logits}_i = \text{LN}_{\text{final}}(\mathbf{x}_i^{(N)}) \cdot Ulogitsi​=LNfinal​(xi(N)​)⋅U

where UUU is the unembedding matrix.

The entire model is just this: embed, loop over blocks that read from and write to the residual stream, then unembed.

Look at the main loop: for block in self.blocks: x = block(x, mask=mask). Because each block returns x + attn_out + ffn_out, the variable x is a running sum. That's the residual stream. After all blocks, x contains the embedding plus every additive contribution from every attention and FFN block in the model. That's what gets projected to vocabulary logits.

One consequence you can test right now: what happens if you delete a layer? In a plain stack, removing any layer disconnects the pipeline. In a residual stack, you just lose one correction.

Misconceptions

"Each layer transforms the representation." Each layer adds to the representation. The difference is subtle but important. "Transforms" implies the layer replaces its input with something new. "Adds to" makes clear that the input persists. The representation at layer 10 still contains the original embedding, unless subsequent layers have happened to cancel it out (which they generally don't).

"Skip connections are just a training trick." In the original ResNet paper, skip connections were motivated as a fix for vanishing gradients, basically a training convenience. In the transformer, the skip connections are the architecture. The entire model is organized around additive updates to a shared stream. Removing them wouldn't give you a harder-to-train version of the same model; it would give you a fundamentally different (and much worse) architecture.

"Deeper layers have completely different representations." Because of the additive structure, early features persist into later layers. Probing experiments consistently find that you can decode surface-level features (token identity, position, part of speech) from the residual stream at any depth. The later layers have added higher-level features on top, but they haven't erased the earlier ones. The residual stream is an accumulator, not a pipeline.

"The residual stream is a single vector for the whole sequence." It's one vector per position. Each token has its own residual stream that flows through the layers. What lets positions talk to each other is the attention sub-layer, which reads from every position's stream and writes back into each one. FFN blocks are position-independent; attention is what mixes across positions.

What's next

This post laid out the central organizing idea: a transformer is a residual stream, and every block is a reader-and-writer on that shared bus. The rest of Arc 4 is about what specific components read from the stream and what they write back.

Additional reading (and watching)

  • Elhage, N., et al. (2021). A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits. Anthropic. The canonical statement of the residual-stream framing and the starting point for mechanistic interpretability.
  • He, K., Zhang, X., Ren, S., & Sun, J. (2015). Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition. CVPR 2016. The original ResNet paper that made very deep networks trainable.
  • Veit, A., Wilber, M., & Belongie, S. (2016). Residual Networks Behave Like Ensembles of Relatively Shallow Networks. NeurIPS 2016. The "unrolled paths" perspective on ResNets that foreshadows the residual-stream framing.
  • Vaswani, A., et al. (2017). Attention Is All You Need. NeurIPS 2017. Introduced the transformer, with residual connections baked into every sub-layer.
  • Elhage, N., et al. (2022). Toy Models of Superposition. Anthropic. The superposition hypothesis for how the residual stream packs more features than it has dimensions.
  • Fan, A., Grave, E., & Joulin, A. (2019). Reducing Transformer Depth on Demand with Structured Dropout. ICLR 2020. LayerDrop; exploits the redundancy implicit in residual-stream architectures.
  • Tenney, I., Das, D., & Pavlick, E. (2019). BERT Rediscovers the Classical NLP Pipeline. ACL 2019. Probing classifiers show that surface-level features are decodable from every layer of a transformer.
  • Xiong, R., et al. (2020). On Layer Normalization in the Transformer Architecture. ICML 2020. Analyzes Pre-LN vs. Post-LN and explains why Pre-LN training is more stable.
  • Lindsey, J., et al. (2025). On the Biology of a Large Language Model. Anthropic. Traces specific computations through Claude's residual stream using attribution graphs, the most ambitious circuit-tracing work to date and the basis for the open-source Circuit Tracer.
  • Templeton, A., et al. (2024). Scaling Monosemanticity: Extracting Interpretable Features from Claude 3 Sonnet. Anthropic. Sparse autoencoders trained on the residual stream of a production frontier model, recovering millions of interpretable features and demonstrating behavioral steering.
