Arc 12: Governance, Economics & Trajectory

Inference Economics: Cost per Token, Batching, and Caching

18 min read

One intuition about AI costs is that training is the expensive part, and once you've paid for it, inference is basically free. Tokens are fractions of a cent. How expensive can they really get?

Expensive enough that a lot of mature AI products spend more on inference in a single good quarter than they spent on training the model they're serving. The one-time cost of pretraining shows up as a single tall spike on an org's P&L. Inference shows up as a bar that grows every month, scales with every new user, and never stops.

This post is about the shape of that inference bar. Where the money actually goes, what knobs move it, and how to build a cost model you can carry in your head.

Why inference dominates spend

Training a frontier model is a big number. A recent pretrain of a 70B-class model runs somewhere in the tens of millions of dollars when you add up compute, data pipeline, salaries, and the failed runs nobody talks about. That number is real. But it's one number, paid once.

Inference is a different animal. Every user request costs something. Every retry costs something. Every agent that fans out into ten tool calls costs ten times something. If your product works and people use it, your inference bill grows linearly with adoption, and then, if you add agentic features, it grows faster than that.

The hero loop above is an illustrative year-one P&L for a successful deployment. The training spike in month 1 looks enormous. By month 12, cumulative inference has passed it, and the inference bar is still climbing while training is done. One way to reframe it: training buys you a model, and inference is the thing you actually sell.

So if you're going to work on production AI systems, a lot of the engineering decisions you'll be asked to make (model selection, prompt design, caching, batching, whether to self-host) are really cost decisions in disguise. Getting the intuition for $/token is the foundation.

Cost per token, decomposed

When a provider quotes "$3 per million tokens," that single number is bundling several things together. Every modern pricing page has at least three numbers, and often four:

  1. Input tokens, uncached. What you pay for the context you send the model, the first time the model sees it.
  2. Input tokens, cached. What you pay for a prefix the provider's serving stack has already processed and kept warm.
  3. Output tokens. What you pay for tokens the model generates. Almost always 4–5× the uncached input price.
  4. Batch-discounted tokens. Some providers offer 30–50% off if you submit work that can be served asynchronously, with latency tolerances of hours.

Rack-rate $/Mtok, illustrative 2026 pricing. Notice two things: output is 4–5x input, and cached input is roughly 10% of uncached. The open-weight row (Llama hosted through a marketplace) collapses all three into one flat number — no input/output asymmetry when you pay for GPU time.

A few things to notice. Output is always much more expensive than input, and that asymmetry is structural. During prefill the model processes the entire input in parallel; attention over TTT tokens is a single big matmul that saturates the GPU. During decode the model has to run the full forward pass once per output token, autoregressively, reading the whole KV cache each time. The FLOP count per token is similar, but the wall-clock cost diverges because decode is bottlenecked on memory bandwidth while prefill is bottlenecked on compute.

Cached input, where it's offered, is usually somewhere between 8% and 25% of the uncached price. The provider has already done the prefill work and saved the intermediate KV states; you're paying for the lookup and the extension, not the whole thing over again. If you're sending the same 20k-token system prompt to every request, this is the single biggest lever you have.

The open-weight row at the bottom collapses everything into one flat number. When you pay for GPU time directly (through a hosted inference marketplace or your own hardware), input and output stop being separate line items.[^1] You're paying for the seconds the hardware is busy, regardless of which phase is consuming them.

The math of a cached prompt

Here's the cost model for input tokens when part of the prompt can be cached:

Let's work a number. Suppose you have a RAG application with a 20k-token input per query (big system prompt, a few retrieved chunks, a short user message), and you're paying $3/Mtok uncached and $0.30/Mtok cached. If the system prompt is 16k of those tokens and always identical, you can expect h≈16000/20000=0.80h \approx 16000 / 20000 = 0.80h≈16000/20000=0.80. Then:

You cut your input bill by 72% without changing anything about the model or the quality of the output. That's essentially the entire value proposition of prompt caching.[^2]

The slider above lets you feel the shape of this curve. Every percentage point of cache hit rate is worth about 2.7 cents per million input tokens at current prices. At scale, those cents are real money. A workload doing 100M input tokens per day at a 0% hit rate costs $300/day. At 80%, it costs $84/day. At 95%, it costs about $44/day.

One thing to flag: the first request that writes to the cache costs slightly more than an uncached request (typically 25% more), because the provider has to store the KV state.[^3] So caching is not a free lunch for one-shot traffic. It pays off when the cached prefix is reused at least 3–4 times before it expires, which is the norm for system prompts but not for every workload.

Quantization: the same model for 25¢ on the dollar

The other big lever, especially if you're running open-weight models, is quantization: representing the model's weights (and sometimes activations) in fewer bits than the 16 they were trained at. 8-bit is nearly free in quality terms on most workloads.[^4] 4-bit is where things get interesting: a well-done INT4 or GPTQ quantization of a 70B model runs at roughly 25% of the memory and 2–4× the throughput of the FP16 original, and preserves somewhere between 92% and 98% of quality on standard benchmarks.[^5]

That's a huge cost win. A 70B FP16 model needs two H100s worth of GPU memory. The same model at 4-bit fits comfortably on one, with room to spare for a bigger batch. You've roughly halved your hourly GPU cost and you can serve more concurrent requests from each GPU. The per-token economics on a quantized self-hosted deployment can be 4–8× cheaper than the FP16 baseline.

The catch, and it's a real one, is that quantization quality is workload-dependent. Code generation and long-horizon reasoning degrade more than casual chat. Rare languages degrade more than English. Multi-turn tasks with tight output formatting requirements can quietly fail in ways you won't catch without careful evals.[^5] Quantization is a cost lever, but it's one you have to measure on your own traffic. You don't get to just assume the benchmark number transfers.

The agent loop multiplier

Agent costs add up quickly. An agent is not one model call. It's many. A chat turn consumes a few hundred input tokens and generates a few hundred output tokens. An agent handling the same user request might make 20 or 50 calls, each one carrying the accumulated history of every previous call.

Three workloads, same model, wildly different token counts. A single chat turn is a thin bar. A tool-use agent with 6 calls is roughly 20x that. A deep agent loop (30 steps) approaches 100x. The bars are proportional to total tokens consumed, which is proportional to cost.

The middle bar in that viz is roughly a light tool-use scenario: 6 calls, each with the growing context prepended. The bottom bar is a deep agent loop, something like a coding assistant doing a 30-step task. Notice the multiplier: the tool-use case is about 20× the tokens of single-turn chat, and the agent loop is closer to 100×. This is before any retries or speculative branches.

Two mechanical things drive the explosion. First, every tool call's result gets appended to the context and then passed back in on the next call. The context grows with the square of the number of turns if you're not careful about what you prune. Second, the output at each step is small (a tool call is a few hundred tokens) but the input on the next step is large (the entire running conversation). So agents are input-heavy workloads where cache hit rates matter enormously.

The practical consequence: when someone asks you what an "agent" costs, the answer is almost never the per-token rate. It's the per-token rate times whatever the average loop depth turns out to be on real traffic, which you can't know until you measure it. A safe default budget for a non-trivial agent is to assume 30–50× the token count of a single-turn baseline and work back from there.

Build vs. buy: where the curves cross

Eventually everyone working on a serious deployment asks whether it would be cheaper to self-host.

The answer depends on throughput, and the dependence is steep. A GPU costs the same per hour whether you're feeding it 1 request per minute or 100. At low utilization, your self-hosted cost-per-token is dominated by the fixed cost of an idle GPU sitting in a rack. At high utilization, you're amortizing that fixed cost over a much larger number of tokens, and your cost-per-token drops sharply.

The self-hosted curve is a hyperbola: cost per token drops sharply as utilization rises, because you're spreading a fixed GPU cost over more tokens. The flat API line crosses it at your break-even point. Drag the GPU rate slider to see how hardware cost shifts that crossover.

The orange line is the flat API price; the green line is a simplified self-host curve (GPU hourly rate divided by sustained throughput). For current prices (roughly $3/Mtok API, $2.50/hr for an on-demand H100, a 70B model quantized to hit about 160 tokens/sec), break-even lands somewhere around 30 output tokens per second sustained, which is roughly 5 queries per second of a typical chat workload. Below that, the API is cheaper. Above that, self-hosting wins, and it wins more the higher you push utilization.[^6]

Drag the GPU rate slider and watch the break-even move. Cheaper hardware (reserved instances, spot, on-prem) pushes the break-even point way down. More expensive hardware (B200s, scarce capacity) pushes it up.

This is only the variable-cost picture. The fixed costs of self-hosting are not in the chart: engineering time for the serving stack, on-call rotation, security patches, capacity planning, noisy-neighbor debugging. A reasonable rule of thumb: self-hosting doesn't pencil out financially until you're confident you'll sustain at least 2–3× the break-even throughput, and you have an engineer who wants to own the stack.

Worked example

Let's put all of this together on a concrete workload. A small team has built a RAG-style assistant that:

  • Serves 1,000,000 queries per month.
  • Each query carries a ~4,000-token retrieved context plus a stable 2,000-token system prompt.
  • Each query produces ~400 tokens of output.
  • The system prompt is identical across queries. The retrieved context is mostly unique per query.

At Claude Sonnet 4 pricing ($3/Mtok input, $0.30 cached, $15/Mtok output), and assuming the system prompt gets cached (so hhh for the input overall is roughly 2000/6000≈0.332000 / 6000 \approx 0.332000/6000≈0.33):

  • Input: 6k tokens/query times 1M queries = 6B tokens/mo. Blended input price at 33% hit rate works out to about $2.11/Mtok. Cost: roughly $12,660.
  • Output: 400 tokens/query times 1M queries = 400M tokens/mo at $15/Mtok = $6,000.
  • Total: ~$18,700/month.

Without caching, the same workload costs about $24,000. The cache is worth about $5,300/month, or 22%, at this hit rate. If the retrieved chunks were also stable and cacheable (which they often aren't, but sometimes are), you'd save closer to 60%.

Swap the prices for GPT-5-mini ($0.60 input, $0.15 cached, $2.40 output) and watch the total drop by roughly 6×. The shape of the calculation is fixed. Which model you pick and how well you cache moves the numbers within that shape.

That's the core cost model for a large fraction of production AI deployments right now. Swap the numbers for your workload and you have a budget.

Misconceptions

"Inference is cheap because tokens are cheap." Tokens are cheap per unit. A single response costs a fraction of a cent. The problem is that production systems don't generate a single response; they generate billions. A $0.0001-per-response cost becomes $100,000/month at 1B responses. And that's before agent loops, retries, and retrieval contexts multiply each "response" into dozens of model calls. The relevant question isn't whether the unit price is small. It's whether the unit price times your real traffic is something your business can absorb.

"Self-hosting is always cheaper." Self-hosting is cheaper per token at high sustained utilization. Below that utilization point, you're paying for an idle GPU, which is usually more expensive than paying a provider who is amortizing that same GPU across a thousand tenants. Self-hosting also has hidden fixed costs: engineering time, on-call, capacity planning, security. For most teams most of the time, the API is the right default, and self-hosting becomes cheaper when you have both the volume and the willingness to operate a serving stack.

"Caching only helps if the entire prompt repeats." Prompt caching works on shared prefixes, not exact matches. If the first 16k tokens of every request are identical (your system prompt, your tool schemas, your few-shot examples) and only the final 4k vary per query, the first 16k are served from the cache on every query after the first. You don't need the full prompt to repeat. You only need the prefix to stabilize.[^2]

"Quantization always tanks quality." 8-bit quantization is essentially free on most workloads.[^4] 4-bit loses a few percent of quality on average and substantially more on the hardest long-form tasks. Whether that tradeoff is acceptable depends entirely on what you're building. A customer support bot that's 96% as good as the FP16 baseline for a quarter of the cost is probably a great deal. A code generation tool where that last 4% is the difference between working and hallucinated imports is a different story. Quantization is a cost lever, not a free one. The right move is to evaluate it on your own traffic and decide.

These ideas in the wild

Anthropic's prompt caching breakpoints. Anthropic's API exposes explicit cache breakpoints: you mark a prefix as cacheable by tagging a content block with cache_control, and the serving stack keeps the KV state warm for five minutes (or longer, with an extended TTL). Reads against the cache are 90% cheaper than uncached reads. Writes are 25% more expensive. The reason for the write premium is storage: the provider is keeping your KV state in fast memory until you use it again, which has a real cost. The 5-minute TTL is an implementation detail that leaks into your architecture, because you have to shape traffic so cached prefixes get reused before they expire.[^2]

vLLM and PagedAttention. When you're self-hosting, you usually want vLLM or a similar engine, because the serving stack is what decides whether your GPU runs at 30% utilization or 90%. PagedAttention, the algorithm behind vLLM, treats the KV cache like OS virtual memory: requests get served from a shared pool of KV pages, and prefixes shared across requests get shared pages in physical memory.[^7] The practical effect is that continuous batching and prefix sharing happen automatically. Two concurrent requests with the same system prompt don't each get their own copy of the KV state. They share it. That's free throughput at the engine level.

FlexGen and the offload frontier. For cost-sensitive workloads where latency doesn't matter (batch analysis, overnight summarization), systems like FlexGen push the envelope by offloading weights and KV state across GPU memory, CPU memory, and disk, and scheduling the computation to hide the transfer latency.[^8] This lets you run a 175B-parameter model on a single commodity GPU, at dramatically lower cost per token than any online serving stack can hit. The tradeoff is latency: token-per-second throughput is one or two orders of magnitude worse than a GPU-resident deployment. Whether that tradeoff is acceptable depends on whether you're serving an interactive product or a batch pipeline.

What's next

Inference economics is only half the picture. The other half is whether you can even use a given model the way you want to: what you're allowed to do with the weights, what data the provider can do with your inputs, and how portable your workload is if the vendor changes terms or goes away. That's where open and closed ecosystems start pulling in different directions, and the answer shapes both your cost model and your risk model.
