Arc 4: Transformers from First Principles

A Close Reading of ‘Attention Is All You Need’

18 min read

What the 2017 paper specified versus what today's transformer actually looks like.

Why read a paper from 2017?

It's easy to work with LLMs in 2026 without ever having read "Attention Is All You Need". The architecture diagram has been redrawn a hundred times in tutorials and blog posts, and most of us have absorbed the ideas secondhand. That's fine for getting work done. But there's a difference between knowing what a transformer is and understanding which parts of the original design were deliberate architectural choices versus which parts were just the thing that happened to work for machine translation in 2017.

Every model you interact with today descends from this paper. GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, Llama, Mistral. All of them. And yet the paper itself describes none of those things. It describes a machine translation system. It uses an encoder-decoder architecture that most modern LLMs have abandoned. Its specific hyperparameters, activation functions, and normalization scheme have all been replaced.

So the interesting question isn't "what does the paper say?" It's "what was essential and what was contingent?" Which ideas turned out to be load-bearing, and which were scaffolding that got removed once the building was up?

And there's a second reason to read it. The paper is short. Fifteen pages including references. That's unusually compact for something this consequential. It's well-written. The notation is consistent. The ablations are informative. It's genuinely a good paper to read, not just an important one.

I'm going to go through it section by section. I'll connect each piece to what we've already built up in this arc, and I'll try to be honest about what the authors got right, what they couldn't have known, and what's quietly misleading if you read it with 2026 eyes.

Prerequisites

This post assumes you've worked through most of Arc 4. Specifically, you should be comfortable with the residual stream (Post 4.1), attention from scratch (Post 4.2), multi-head attention (Post 4.3), causal masking (Post 4.4), positional encodings and RoPE (Post 4.5), FFN as memory (Post 4.6), layer norms (Post 4.7), and the decoder-only vs. encoder-decoder distinction (Post 4.8). You don't need to remember every detail. But you should have the mental model of how these pieces fit together, because this post is about tracing each piece back to its origin in the 2017 paper.

Section 1: Introduction

The paper opens with a clear argument: recurrent models are fundamentally sequential, and that sequentiality prevents parallelization.

In 2017, the state of the art for machine translation was RNNs augmented with attention. Bahdanau et al. (2015) had shown that you could let a decoder attend to encoder hidden states rather than compressing the entire source sentence into a single vector. Luong et al. refined the attention mechanism further. These models worked well. But they had a structural problem: because each hidden state depends on the previous one, you can't compute them in parallel. For long sequences on a GPU, that's a huge bottleneck.

If you've read Post 2.7 on seq2seq and Bahdanau attention, you've seen this world. The encoder processes the source sentence left to right, building up hidden states one at a time. The decoder does the same thing. Attention helped with the information bottleneck (the decoder could look back at all encoder states, not just the final one), but it didn't fix the sequential dependency. You still couldn't parallelize the recurrent computation.

The transformer's argument was simple: replace the recurrence entirely. Use self-attention to let every position interact with every other position in a single operation. The entire sequence gets processed in one parallel step.

The paper skips Section 2 (just a brief background on existing attention mechanisms) and jumps to the architecture in Section 3. But before we go there, I want to note something about the introduction's framing. The authors position the transformer primarily as a speed improvement. "The Transformer allows for significantly more parallelization and can reach a new state of the art in translation quality after being trained for as little as twelve hours on eight P100 GPUs." Twelve hours on eight GPUs. That was the pitch.

What the paper didn't fully articulate, and maybe couldn't have known at the time, was that this parallelism argument went far beyond training speed. It turned out to be the key to scaling. When you scale a model from 100M to 100B parameters, you need to distribute computation across thousands of GPUs, and recurrent architectures are fundamentally hostile to that kind of parallelism in a way self-attention is not. So the transformer didn't only train faster. It was the only architecture that could actually exploit the hardware trajectory that was already underway. The authors were making a practical argument about wall-clock time on 2017 hardware, and the actual consequence was that they'd built the only architecture that could ride the compute scaling curve of the next decade.

Section 3: Model Architecture

This is where the famous Figure 1 lives. If you've spent any time in the LLM world, you've seen it: the stacked encoder and decoder blocks with their boxes labeled "Multi-Head Attention," "Add & Norm," and "Feed Forward."

Let me map each component to what we've already covered in this arc.

Multi-Head Attention is exactly what we built in Post 4.2 and Post 4.3. The paper describes it in Section 3.2 and 3.3, and we'll get to the details in those sections below.

"Add & Norm" is the residual connection (the "Add") followed by layer normalization (the "Norm"). The residual stream we discussed in Post 4.1 is the Add part. Layer normalization is from Post 4.7. There's an important subtlety here that I'll come back to: the paper uses post-norm (normalize after the residual addition), but virtually every modern model uses pre-norm (normalize before the sublayer). This turned out to matter a lot for training stability. The paper even has a figure that shows the post-norm placement, and it's arguably the most quietly misleading part of the diagram if you're trying to understand how modern models work.

Feed Forward is the position-wise FFN we covered in Post 4.6. The paper uses ReLU; modern models use SwiGLU. We'll look at the exact formulation below.

Positional Encoding appears at the bottom of the diagram, added to the input embeddings. This is Post 4.5, where we traced the path from sinusoidal encodings to RoPE.

Embedding and output layers. The paper mentions something that's easy to miss: they share the weight matrix between the two embedding layers (source and target) and the pre-softmax linear transformation. Weight tying. This reduces the total parameter count and provides a useful inductive bias (the output projection lives in the same space as the input embeddings). Most modern models also tie input embeddings to the output projection, so this detail survived.

Now here's the part that's easy to misread when you look at Figure 1 with 2026 eyes. The paper describes an encoder-decoder model. There are two stacks: an encoder (left side) that processes the source sentence, and a decoder (right side) that generates the target sentence one token at a time. The decoder has an additional attention layer that attends to the encoder's output. This is called cross-attention.

This is NOT the architecture that GPT, Claude, or Llama use. Those are decoder-only models (we covered the distinction in Post 4.8). They have no encoder. They have no cross-attention. They just have the right side of the diagram, with the causal mask from Post 4.4 preventing future positions from being seen.

The original transformer was designed to translate German to English. The encoder reads "Ich bin ein Berliner" and the decoder produces "I am a Berliner" one token at a time. Radford et al. (2018) showed that if you strip out the encoder, keep only the masked decoder, and train it on a language modeling objective, you get GPT. That architectural simplification turned out to be one of the most consequential decisions in the whole lineage. But it came a year later, and it's not in this paper.

Section 3.2: Scaled Dot-Product Attention

Here's the core equation:

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QKTdk)V\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)VAttention(Q,K,V)=softmax(dk​​QKT​)V

If you've read Post 4.2, this is old news. Queries dot-producted with keys, scaled, softmaxed, then used to weight values. But there are a few details in this section worth pausing on.

First, the paper includes a footnote comparing additive attention (the kind Bahdanau used) to dot-product attention. They note that additive attention "theoretically has more expressive power" because it uses a learned weight matrix and a tanh nonlinearity rather than a simple dot product. But dot-product attention is much faster in practice because it can be implemented as a single matrix multiply, which GPUs are extremely good at. They chose speed over expressiveness.

This turned out not to matter. The dot-product formulation has been universal since 2017. Nobody uses additive attention in transformers. The expressiveness concern was theoretical, and the speed advantage was decisive. I think there's a broader lesson here: in deep learning, the faster formulation often wins even when the slower one is theoretically more powerful, because the faster one can be trained on more data in the same wall-clock time. Speed and expressiveness aren't independent axes. Speed translates into more training, which compensates for lower per-operation expressiveness.

Second, the dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ scaling. The paper says: "We suspect that for large values of dkd_kdk​, the dot products grow large in magnitude, pushing the softmax function into regions where it has extremely small gradients." They're saying that without scaling, the softmax saturates: one score dominates and the gradients vanish. This is exactly the variance argument we worked through in Post 4.2. If QQQ and KKK have entries with zero mean and unit variance, the dot product Q⋅KQ \cdot KQ⋅K has variance dkd_kdk​. Dividing by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ brings it back to unit variance, which keeps the softmax in the region where its gradients are healthy.

I like that they said "we suspect" rather than "we prove." The justification is a back-of-the-envelope variance calculation, not a formal theorem. And it was exactly right. The scaling factor has persisted unchanged for nearly a decade. Sometimes the back-of-the-envelope answer is the one that matters.

Third, there's a small point about the choice of softmax itself. The paper doesn't discuss alternatives to softmax for computing attention weights. In 2017, this seemed obvious. But by 2023-2024, researchers were exploring linear attention (which drops the softmax entirely and replaces it with other kernel functions) to avoid the quadratic cost. The softmax is what forces the full n×nn \times nn×n matrix to be materialized. Without it, you can rewrite the attention computation to avoid the quadratic dependency on sequence length. None of those alternatives have displaced softmax attention in practice (FlashAttention made softmax attention fast enough to not need replacing), but the paper's implicit assumption that "of course you use softmax" turned out to be a more interesting design choice than it seemed at the time.

Section 3.3: Multi-Head Attention

The paper proposes 8 heads, with dk=dv=64d_k = d_v = 64dk​=dv​=64 and dmodel=512d_\text{model} = 512dmodel​=512. So each head operates on a 64-dimensional subspace, and there are 8 of them, which accounts for the full 512-dimensional model.

The key quote: "Multi-head attention allows the model to jointly attend to information from different representation subspaces at different positions. With a single attention head, averaging inhibits this."

That sentence is doing a lot of work. What they're saying is that a single attention head produces a single set of attention weights. Every dimension of the value vector gets weighted by the same distribution. If token 3 attends strongly to token 7, it attends strongly to token 7 across all dimensions. Multiple heads let different subsets of dimensions attend to different positions. Maybe one head tracks syntactic relationships while another tracks semantic ones. We covered this in detail in Post 4.3.

What the paper didn't explore: the idea that you don't need separate key and value heads for each query head. Multi-Query Attention (MQA) shares a single K/V head across all query heads. Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) shares K/V heads among groups of query heads. These weren't efficiency tricks that happened to work; they were possible because the K and V projections turn out to be more redundant across heads than the Q projections. We covered those variants in Post 4.3 as well.

Modern models have moved to 32, 64, or even 128 heads. The 8-head design was just what worked for the base model size. The concept was right. The hyperparameters were context-specific.

There's also a small ablation in the paper (Table 3) that's easy to skip over but is informative. They tried 1, 4, 8, 16, and 32 heads. The single-head model performed notably worse. Going from 8 to 16 heads helped a little. Going from 16 to 32 helped a tiny bit. They concluded that 8 was a good balance for their model size. The diminishing returns with more heads at the same dmodeld_\text{model}dmodel​ make sense: each head gets a smaller subspace to work with. At dmodel=512d_\text{model} = 512dmodel​=512 with 32 heads, each head only has 16 dimensions, which constrains the kinds of attention patterns it can express. Modern models get away with many more heads because they also have much larger dmodeld_\text{model}dmodel​.

Section 3.4: Position-wise Feed-Forward Networks

The paper's FFN formulation is clean:

FFN(x)=max⁡(0,  xW1+b1) W2+b2\text{FFN}(x) = \max(0, \; xW_1 + b_1) \, W_2 + b_2FFN(x)=max(0,xW1​+b1​)W2​+b2​

That max⁡(0,⋅)\max(0, \cdot)max(0,⋅) is ReLU. The input dimension is dmodel=512d_\text{model} = 512dmodel​=512, the inner dimension is dff=2048d_{ff} = 2048dff​=2048 (a 4x expansion), and then it projects back down to 512. This is applied identically to each position, hence "position-wise." Every token gets the same two-layer neural network applied to it independently.

Both the activation function and the expansion ratio have been replaced in modern models. Here's what that looks like concretely:

The SwiGLU variant (from Shazeer, 2020) turned out to give better quality at the same parameter count. The gating mechanism lets the network learn to selectively suppress parts of the hidden representation, which ReLU can only do at zero (it's either on or off). SwiGLU has a smooth gate that can partially suppress, which gives the optimizer more to work with.

Notice that the modern version also drops the bias terms. The original FFN has biases (b1b_1b1​ and b2b_2b2​). Modern FFN blocks typically don't. This is a micro-optimization: biases add a small number of parameters and have essentially no measurable impact on quality at large scale. Dropping them simplifies the implementation and saves a few bytes per parameter. Not important individually, but a pattern that shows how the field got more systematic about trimming unnecessary components.

The position-wise nature of the FFN is the part that really endured. Geva et al. (2021) later showed that you can interpret these FFN layers as key-value memories, where the first projection's rows are "keys" that match input patterns and the second projection's columns are "values" that get written to the residual stream. We covered this interpretation in Post 4.6. The paper doesn't mention this at all. The authors describe the FFN as simply a "position-wise fully connected feed-forward network" and move on. But the architecture they chose happened to create the structure that supports this memory interpretation. It's a case where the right design was chosen for the wrong reasons (or at least for reasons unrelated to the deeper interpretation that came later).

Section 3.5: Positional Encoding

The paper proposes sinusoidal positional encodings:

PE(pos,2i)=sin⁡(pos100002i/dmodel),PE(pos,2i+1)=cos⁡(pos100002i/dmodel)PE_{(pos, 2i)} = \sin\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_\text{model}}}\right), \quad PE_{(pos, 2i+1)} = \cos\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_\text{model}}}\right)PE(pos,2i)​=sin(100002i/dmodel​pos​),PE(pos,2i+1)​=cos(100002i/dmodel​pos​)

Each position gets a unique vector, and these vectors are added to the token embeddings at the bottom of the model. The intuition is that each frequency captures periodicity at a different scale, and the model can learn to attend to relative positions by learning linear functions of these sinusoids.

The paper also tried learned absolute position embeddings and found "nearly identical results" (Table 3, row E). They went with sinusoidal because it might generalize to longer sequences than seen during training. That hope turned out to be partially misplaced. Sinusoidal encodings don't extrapolate well in practice. You can train on sequences up to length 512 and then try to use them at length 1024, and the model tends to fall apart. The mathematical property (that relative positions can be expressed as linear functions of the sinusoidal encodings) exists, but the model doesn't learn to exploit it robustly.

Both approaches have been replaced. In Post 4.5, we traced the evolution from these absolute schemes to RoPE (Rotary Position Embeddings), which encodes position by rotating the query and key vectors rather than adding a fixed vector to the input. The crucial difference is where the position information enters the computation. Sinusoidal encodings add position to the embeddings before any attention is computed. RoPE applies position directly to the Q and K vectors inside the attention operation. This means RoPE naturally captures relative position (the attention score between two tokens depends only on the distance between them, not their absolute positions), which is the property the paper's authors were hoping sinusoidal encodings would provide. RoPE actually delivers on that promise, and it's compatible with KV caching, which sinusoidal encodings bolted onto the input are not particularly friendly to.

There's a nice bit of prescience in Section 3.5, though. The authors write that they hypothesized sinusoidal encodings would "allow the model to easily learn to attend by relative positions, since for any fixed offset kkk, PEpos+kPE_{pos+k}PEpos+k​ can be represented as a linear function of PEposPE_{pos}PEpos​." The intuition was right. The mechanism just wasn't quite the right one to realize it.

Section 4: Why Self-Attention

This is the section where the paper makes its theoretical case. Table 1 compares self-attention against recurrent layers and convolutional layers across three criteria: computational complexity per layer, sequential operations required, and maximum path length between any two positions.

Self-attention has O(n2⋅d)O(n^2 \cdot d)O(n2⋅d) computation per layer, O(1)O(1)O(1) sequential operations, and O(1)O(1)O(1) maximum path length. A recurrent layer has O(n⋅d2)O(n \cdot d^2)O(n⋅d2) computation per layer, O(n)O(n)O(n) sequential operations, and O(n)O(n)O(n) maximum path length.

The O(1)O(1)O(1) path length argument is the fundamental one. In an RNN, information from token 0 has to propagate through nnn hidden states to reach token nnn. At each step, it gets multiplied by weight matrices, potentially losing signal through vanishing gradients. In self-attention, token 0 and token nnn interact directly in a single hop. The information doesn't have to traverse the whole sequence.

This is worth sitting with for a moment. The RNN's sequential path length isn't just a theoretical concern. It's the reason RNNs struggle with long-range dependencies even when they have enough parameters. Information degrades as it passes through repeated matrix multiplications. LSTMs mitigated this with gating mechanisms (we covered those in the pre-transformer arc), but they couldn't eliminate it. The transformer's O(1)O(1)O(1) path length doesn't just mean "faster." It means that the gradient signal from a relationship between token 0 and token 500 is just as strong as the signal from a relationship between token 499 and token 500. Every dependency, regardless of distance, gets the same direct connection.

The paper also includes convolutions in the comparison, which is easy to forget in 2026. In 2017, convolutional approaches to sequence modeling (like ConvS2S from Facebook) were a serious contender. The paper argues that convolutions require O(log⁡kn)O(\log_k n)O(logk​n) layers to connect two positions separated by nnn tokens, compared to the O(1)O(1)O(1) of self-attention. Convolutions for NLP have essentially disappeared since then, so this part of the argument is historically interesting but practically moot.

The paper acknowledges the O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) cost but doesn't seem particularly worried about it. The authors note that for "typical" sequence lengths used in machine translation, the n2n^2n2 term is smaller than d2d^2d2 because sentences are usually shorter than the model dimension. They even propose a restricted self-attention variant that only attends to a local neighborhood of size rrr (reducing complexity to O(n⋅r)O(n \cdot r)O(n⋅r)), but they don't use it in their experiments.

With hindsight, the O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) cost became the defining scaling challenge. When context lengths grew from 512 tokens (the paper's setting) to 128K or even 1M tokens, that quadratic scaling went from negligible to dominant. It motivated an entire research field: FlashAttention (an exact algorithm that reduces memory access, not the complexity itself), sparse attention patterns, linear attention approximations, and the various long-context tricks that let modern models handle book-length inputs. The authors were right that the quadratic cost wasn't a problem for their use case. They just couldn't have anticipated that the use case would change so dramatically.

There's something almost poetic about that. The very feature that made the transformer work (every token can attend to every other token) is also the feature that creates its most pressing engineering challenge at scale. The O(1)O(1)O(1) path length and the O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) cost are two sides of the same coin.

Section 6: Results

The paper's results section is about machine translation, so the specific numbers aren't super relevant to us. But a few things stand out.

On WMT 2014 English-to-German, the "big" transformer model achieved 28.4 BLEU, a new state of the art. On English-to-French, it hit 41.0 BLEU. Both results surpassed the best existing models, including ensembles of RNNs. And here's the kicker: the big transformer was trained in 3.5 days on 8 P100 GPUs. The previous state-of-the-art models took weeks to train. So the transformer was both better and cheaper to train, and that combination is rare. It's usually what causes an architecture to take over.

The ablation study in Table 3 is worth reading carefully. They systematically vary the number of heads, the attention key dimension, the model size, the dropout rate, and the positional encoding scheme. The results confirm that multi-head attention matters (single-head is worse), that model size matters (bigger is better, unsurprisingly), and that the positional encoding scheme barely matters (sinusoidal vs. learned gives "nearly identical results," as I mentioned above).

One thing I notice reading the ablations: the differences between configurations are small. A BLEU point here, a BLEU point there. The architecture is pretty robust to these hyperparameter choices. That robustness may be part of why the transformer was so easy to adapt to new tasks. You don't have to get the details exactly right. The core mechanism is forgiving.

What survived

Let me try to separate the load-bearing parts from the contingent ones.

Scaled dot-product attention. The exact formula from Section 3.2, including the dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ scaling, is still used in every transformer I'm aware of. FlashAttention changed how it's computed at the hardware level, but the mathematical operation is unchanged.

Multi-head attention. The concept of splitting the representation into parallel attention heads, each operating on a subspace, has persisted. The number of heads changed, and the K/V sharing scheme changed (GQA/MQA), but the multi-head principle is still there.

Residual connections. The "Add" in "Add & Norm" is still exactly the same: x+sublayer(x)x + \text{sublayer}(x)x+sublayer(x). The residual stream as the backbone of information flow has only become more central to how we understand transformers, especially through the circuits framework from Elhage et al.

The position-wise FFN block. Two linear projections with a nonlinearity, applied independently at each position. The nonlinearity changed. The expansion ratio changed. A gating mechanism was added. But the structural role of the FFN block within each transformer layer is the same.

The overall layer structure. Attention, then FFN, repeated NNN times. The paper stacked 6 layers. Modern models stack 32, 80, or more. But the alternating attention-FFN structure, with residual connections around each sublayer, is unchanged.

The Q/K/V formulation. The idea that the input is linearly projected into three separate roles (query, key, value) has been surprisingly durable. This is one of those things that seems like it could have gone a different way, but didn't. You could imagine architectures where Q and K are the same (symmetric attention), or where there's no separate V projection (just use the input directly as the value). People have tried these simplifications. They're worse. The three-role decomposition seems to be genuinely important for the model's expressiveness.

The training recipe structure. Adam optimizer, warmup schedule, residual dropout. The specific numbers have changed, but the general recipe (Adam-family optimizer, warmup, some form of regularization) has been persistent. AdamW replaced Adam, warmup lengths changed, dropout was dropped for large models, but the skeleton of the training setup from this paper is still recognizable in modern training runs.

What got replaced

I count six major changes between the 2017 architecture and what a typical large language model uses in 2026. Most of these happened between 2019 and 2023. It's a surprisingly short list given how many years have passed.

Post-norm became pre-norm (and then RMSNorm). The paper normalizes after the residual addition: LayerNorm(x+sublayer(x))\text{LayerNorm}(x + \text{sublayer}(x))LayerNorm(x+sublayer(x)). This seems like a small detail, but it turned out to matter a lot. Raschka (2023) has a nice write-up on the problem. With post-norm, the gradient path from the loss to early layers has to pass through every normalization layer. That creates vanishing gradient issues in deep networks. Pre-norm (x+sublayer(Norm(x))x + \text{sublayer}(\text{Norm}(x))x+sublayer(Norm(x))) keeps the residual path completely clean, an unobstructed highway from the top of the network to the bottom. That makes training much more stable, especially for the deep models (32+ layers) that came after the original 6-layer design.

And then RMSNorm replaced LayerNorm because the mean-centering step in LayerNorm turned out to be unnecessary. Just normalizing by the root-mean-square works fine and is faster. We covered this whole trajectory in Post 4.7. The interesting thing is that the paper's authors clearly knew about residual connections from the ResNet literature, but they placed the norm in the position that made the residual path less clean. I suspect this was inherited from convention rather than deliberately chosen.

Sinusoidal encodings became RoPE. As discussed above. Absolute position, whether sinusoidal or learned, has been almost entirely displaced by relative position schemes.

Encoder-decoder became decoder-only. For generative language models, at least. The encoder-decoder structure is still used in some settings (speech recognition, translation, and some multimodal models), but the dominant LLM architecture is decoder-only. The original paper needed both stacks because translation is a two-language problem: you need to encode the source and decode into the target. When the task became "model language itself," the two-stack design was unnecessary complexity. We covered the full set of tradeoffs in Post 4.8.

ReLU became SwiGLU. A strictly better activation function for this setting. ReLU zeros out all negative inputs, which means a large fraction of neurons can become permanently inactive ("dead neurons"). SwiGLU's smooth gating avoids this entirely.

The hyperparameters scaled. dmodeld_\text{model}dmodel​ went from 512 to 4096 or more. Heads went from 8 to 32-128. Layers went from 6 to 32-80+. The expansion factor in the FFN changed to accommodate SwiGLU. None of this is surprising, but the specific numbers in the paper were tuned for WMT English-to-German translation, not for general language modeling.

Standard MHA became GQA/MQA. When the KV cache became a bottleneck at inference time (which the paper didn't discuss at all), the community found that sharing K/V heads across query head groups saved enormous memory with minimal quality loss. This is a good example of a modification driven by a concern the original paper never had to think about. In an encoder-decoder translation model, you generate maybe 50 tokens of output. KV cache size is irrelevant. When you're generating 8,000-token essays or 100,000-token code files, the KV cache can easily consume more memory than the model weights themselves.

What was never in the paper

This is maybe the most interesting part. The paper is about sequence transduction, specifically machine translation. Several things that people associate with "the transformer" aren't in here at all.

Next-token prediction. The training objective in this paper is sequence-to-sequence: given a source sentence, produce a target sentence. The autoregressive language modeling objective (predict the next token given all previous tokens) came from the GPT line of work a year later. This matters because the training objective shapes everything about how the model is used. A translation model takes an input and produces an output. A language model generates from a prompt. The decoder-only transformer with a causal language modeling objective is what enabled chat, code generation, reasoning, and all the things people now associate with "AI." That framing wasn't in this paper.

Scaling laws. Kaplan et al. (2020) showed that transformer loss scales as a power law with model size, dataset size, and compute. This is arguably the most important empirical finding about transformers, and it explains why the field went from training models on 8 GPUs to training them on thousands of GPUs in just a few years. It's not in the 2017 paper. The authors had no particular reason to believe that making the model 100x bigger would yield smoothly predictable improvements.

Few-shot learning and foundation models. GPT-2 showed that large language models could perform tasks they weren't explicitly trained for. GPT-3 turned this into a practical paradigm with in-context learning. None of this was anticipated in the original paper, which evaluated on BLEU scores for translation. The idea that you could give a model a handful of examples in the prompt and it would generalize was a qualitative surprise, not a predictable consequence of the architecture.

KV caching. When you generate tokens autoregressively, the K and V projections for all previous tokens don't change. So you can cache them and only compute K and V for the new token at each step. This is fundamental to efficient inference, and it follows naturally from the architecture. But the paper doesn't mention it, probably because their focus was on training speed, not inference efficiency. We'll cover KV caching in detail when we get to Arc 5.

The word "language model." I find this striking. The paper's abstract says "sequence transduction models." It talks about "translation" and "parsing." The paper is not trying to build a language model. It's trying to build a better translation model. The idea that this architecture would become the universal backbone for language modeling, code generation, reasoning, multimodal understanding, and agent systems was not on the table.

Dropout as the primary regularizer. The paper uses dropout at a rate of 0.1 on attention weights and sublayer outputs. Modern large models typically don't use dropout at all, relying instead on the regularizing effect of enormous datasets and other techniques. When your training set is the internet, overfitting is a different kind of problem.

What I find interesting about this list is that every replacement was driven by a specific practical problem that surfaced at scale. Pre-norm fixed training instability in deep networks. RoPE fixed length generalization. Decoder-only simplified the architecture for language modeling. SwiGLU improved quality per parameter. GQA reduced memory cost at inference. None of these were changes to the fundamental computational structure. They were refinements to the supporting machinery around the core attention operation.

The annotated versions

If you want to go deeper, two resources from 2018 remain excellent.

Alexander Rush's "The Annotated Transformer" is a line-by-line reimplementation of the paper in PyTorch. It's pedagogically great because it maps every equation to working code. The original version used PyTorch 0.3; it's been updated for modern PyTorch. If you want to understand the implementation details that the paper glosses over (like how masking is applied in practice, or how the learning rate schedule works), this is where to go.

Jay Alammar's "The Illustrated Transformer" is the visualization-first version. If you're a visual thinker, Alammar's step-by-step diagrams of how Q, K, and V interact, how multi-head attention splits and recombines, and how the encoder and decoder connect are probably clearer than anything I could write here. His blog was one of the resources that got me into this whole area.

Both of these describe the original encoder-decoder architecture from the paper, not the decoder-only architecture that modern LLMs use. That's worth keeping in mind. If you read The Annotated Transformer and then try to map it onto how Llama works, you'll notice the encoder half is missing entirely, and the cross-attention layer in the decoder is gone too. But the self-attention and FFN blocks in the decoder are the same operations, just with different normalization and activations.

A note on training details

Section 5 of the paper describes the training regime. I won't go through it exhaustively, but a few things are worth flagging.

The learning rate schedule is unusual. They use a warmup period where the learning rate increases linearly, then decreases proportionally to the inverse square root of the step number. The paper defines this as:

lr=dmodel−0.5⋅min⁡(step−0.5,  step⋅warmup_steps−1.5)lr = d_\text{model}^{-0.5} \cdot \min(\text{step}^{-0.5}, \; \text{step} \cdot \text{warmup\_steps}^{-1.5})lr=dmodel−0.5​⋅min(step−0.5,step⋅warmup_steps−1.5)

This "Noam scheduler" (named after Noam Shazeer, second author... the scheduler got informally named after him anyway) became widely used in transformer training for a while. Modern training runs tend to use cosine annealing or other schedules, but the idea of a warmup phase persisted. Training a transformer with a high learning rate from step zero tends to diverge. The warmup gives the model a chance to find a reasonable region of parameter space before the learning rate gets aggressive.

The paper's Noam schedule (warmup then inverse-square-root decay) vs. the modern warmup-plus-cosine schedule that almost every large pretrain uses today. Both have a warmup ramp, which is the part that survived. The decay shape is what changed: inverse-sqrt cools off forever; cosine lands at a fixed minimum learning rate at the end of training.

They also use label smoothing with a value of 0.1. Instead of training against hard one-hot targets, they soften the target distribution so the correct token gets 90% probability and the remaining 10% is spread uniformly across all other tokens. This hurts perplexity (because the model can never be maximally confident about the right answer) but improves BLEU score and accuracy. It's a regularization technique. Modern LLMs have mostly moved away from label smoothing, but it shows up in various places.

So what's the legacy?

I think the paper's lasting contribution is the computational primitive itself: self-attention as a general-purpose mechanism for sequence processing. The idea that you can replace sequential recurrence with a single parallel operation that lets every position interact with every other position. That was the breakthrough, and almost everything else in the paper has been replaced.

Everything built on top of that primitive has been modified, optimized, replaced, and scaled beyond what the original authors were working with. The normalization scheme changed. The position encoding changed. The activation function changed. The overall architecture simplified from encoder-decoder to decoder-only. The training objective shifted from translation to next-token prediction. The scale grew by three orders of magnitude.

But the core operation (computing attention weights from queries and keys, using those weights to aggregate values, doing this in parallel across multiple heads) hasn't changed. Elhage et al. (2021) formalized this into a mathematical framework for understanding transformer circuits, and even their analysis is built directly on the operations described in Section 3.2 of this paper.

I also want to note something about the paper's authorship. Eight authors from Google Brain and Google Research. Several of them went on to found AI companies (Vaswani co-founded Adept and then Essential AI; Shazeer co-founded Character.AI). The paper has been cited over 100,000 times. It's one of the most influential papers in the history of computer science by any quantitative measure. It was posted to arXiv in June 2017 and accepted at NeurIPS that same year.

Reading the paper in 2026 is a useful exercise in separating the permanent from the temporary. The permanent part is surprisingly small: one equation, one pattern (multi-head), one structural principle (alternating attention and FFN with residual connections). Everything else was reasonable for 2017 and has since been improved. That's not a criticism of the paper. That's how good research works. You get the core idea right, and the details get refined by the thousands of researchers who come after you.

If you've been following along through this arc, you've already built up every piece of the transformer from first principles. This post was about tracing those pieces back to where they first appeared, and understanding which ones were the invention and which ones were the context. We started the arc by building the residual stream, then layered in attention, multi-head splitting, causal masking, positional encodings, FFN blocks, normalization, and the encoder-decoder distinction. The paper introduced most of these ideas in a single document. We took nine posts to unpack them.

One last thought. The paper's title is "Attention Is All You Need." In 2017, that was a bold claim against a field dominated by recurrence and convolution. In 2026, it reads almost like prophecy. Self-attention really is all you need, in the sense that every major language model uses it as the primary mechanism for inter-token communication. The supporting cast changed. The star of the show didn't.

Misconceptions

"The transformer paper invented attention." It didn't. Bahdanau et al. (2015) introduced attention for neural machine translation two years earlier. The paper's contribution was showing that attention alone, without recurrence, could be the entire computation. The title "Attention Is All You Need" is a claim about architecture, not a claim about novelty. Self-attention as a sequence-processing primitive (rather than an add-on to an RNN) was the new idea.

"Modern LLMs use the architecture from this paper." They use the computational primitives from this paper, but the architecture itself has been significantly modified. The encoder half is gone. The normalization placement is reversed. The activation function is different. The positional encoding scheme is different. The KV sharing scheme is different. Saying "GPT-4 uses the transformer architecture" is roughly as precise as saying a 2026 car uses the internal combustion engine from 1886. The core principle survived; the implementation details didn't.

"Transformers replaced RNNs because they were more accurate." This one is half right. In the paper, the transformer beats the best RNN-based systems on BLEU, so accuracy is part of the story. But if it had only been a 1 BLEU bump on translation, the architecture would have been a footnote. What killed RNNs was parallelism. The transformer trained in 3.5 days where its predecessors took weeks, and that gap widens further as you scale up. Once the field figured out it could throw 10x more compute at the same wall-clock training budget, the RNN was structurally locked out of the race.

MoE is the big architectural change since the paper that doesn't fit neatly on the "what got replaced" list, because it's not a one-for-one swap.   • Vaswani, A., et al. (2017). Attention Is All You Need. NeurIPS 2017. The paper this post is a close reading of. Pitched as a faster translation model; became the architecture behind every frontier LLM.
  • Rush, A. (2018). The Annotated Transformer. Harvard NLP. Line-by-line PyTorch reimplementation of the paper — the best single resource for the implementation details the paper glosses over.
  • Alammar, J. (2018). The Illustrated Transformer. Visualization-first walkthrough of Q/K/V and multi-head attention. Still the clearest visual introduction to the architecture.
  • Raschka, S. (2023). About LayerNorm Variants in the Original Transformer Paper. Clean explanation of why post-norm was replaced by pre-norm, and why the original diagram is quietly misleading.
  • Radford, A., et al. (2018). Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training. The original GPT paper — the architectural move from encoder-decoder to decoder-only that the Vaswani paper didn't take.
  • Bahdanau, D., Cho, K., & Bengio, Y. (2015). Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate. ICLR 2015. The attention mechanism the transformer was built to generalize — RNN seq2seq with soft alignment over encoder states.
  • Elhage, N., et al. (2021). A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits. Anthropic. Rewrites the paper's attention operation as a mathematical framework for reasoning about what specific heads and layers compute.
  • Dao, T., et al. (2022). FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness. NeurIPS 2022. Same scaled dot-product attention from Section 3.2; faster execution by fusing softmax and matmul into one IO-aware kernel.
  • Shazeer, N. (2020). GLU Variants Improve Transformer. arXiv:2002.05202. The SwiGLU paper; introduced the gated FFN variant that replaced ReLU+two-linear in modern LLMs.
