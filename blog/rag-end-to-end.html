Arc 9: Retrieval, Memory & Context Engineering

RAG Architectures End to End

14 min readbuilds on Embeddings from Scratch: From Word2Vec to E5, Dense, Sparse, and Hybrid Retrieval, Vector Search and Approximate Nearest Neighbors, Chunking Strategies and Rerankers and Cross-Encoders

A note on this series

I built this series as a learning project and used Claude extensively for research, drafting, critique, coding, and revision. I chose the curriculum and dependency order, drove the questions, edited the explanations, developed the interactive examples, and take responsibility for the published result.

This is an educational first edition rather than a formal reference. I am reviewing the articles against primary sources, testing the executable examples, and tracking corrections as the series matures. Some examples require specific hardware, model weights, or external services; they are teaching examples, not a tested deployment recipe.

The previous posts built a search pipeline from embeddings, indexes, chunks, and rerankers. Now we use its results to answer a question. The application must retrieve supporting passages, assemble them into a prompt, and check whether the generated answer follows the evidence.

A basic implementation embeds documents, retrieves the top-k chunks for a query, and inserts those chunks into the prompt. Query rewriting, reranking, and iterative retrieval add steps when that pipeline cannot supply the evidence needed for an answer.

The problem this layer solves

A model’s weights are not a reliable store for your latest handbook, codebase or tickets. Retrieval supplies external evidence at query time without requiring a weight update whenever a document changes.

The application includes the selected passages as reference material in the prompt. Other RAG designs train the retriever or generator too.

When an answer fails, first check whether the retrieved passages contain the needed evidence. Then check how the model used it. That separates retrieval failures from generation failures and helps locate the stage to change.

What this post assumes

The pipeline combines embeddings and vector search (how a chunk becomes a vector), dense-sparse-hybrid (lexical and dense matching signals), vector search and ANN (HNSW, IVF, and friends), chunking strategies (how text becomes chunks), and rerankers (cross-encoders on top-k).

Later we will let the model request retrieval as a tool: it emits a request, the runtime executes the search, and the results return as context. Arc 10 covers that loop in detail.

Naive RAG, and why it breaks

The minimal version embeds the query, retrieves the top-k most similar chunks, and includes them in the prompt with instructions to answer. Similarity alone does not check that a passage covers the right date or product version, or contains the fact the question asks for.

Constructed failures: a wrong-year passage, incompatible document editions, and an unsupported number. These require different repairs; none is diagnosed by fluent wording alone.

A retrieval miss leaves the required evidence outside the candidate set. Similarity can confuse dates or versions even when the general topic matches. The model may notice that mismatch, but it cannot cite a missing passage.

Conflicting context can contain superseded documents or different product editions. Preserve date, version and source metadata so the system can resolve the scope or report the disagreement.

Unsupported generation occurs when the model supplies an answer that the retrieved passages do not establish. Fluent wording can conceal the gap. Returning source passages lets the reader check whether each claim is supported.

Add or change a stage in response to an observed failure. A more elaborate pipeline can create new errors as well as remove old ones.

The full pipeline

Separate the work done when documents arrive from the work done for each query.

The ingestion side runs when your data changes: parse the raw documents, chunk them into passages, embed the passages, write everything into the index along with metadata. This work can be batched and reused across queries. Changes to documents, parsing or the embedding configuration may require re-indexing.

The query side runs per request: embed the query, retrieve top-k from the index, optionally rerank, assemble a prompt with the chosen context, and generate.

Eight constructed stages: parse, chunk, embed, index, retrieve, rerank, assemble and generate. Values are illustrative except the explicitly computed vector payload size. Citation IDs still need claim-support checks.

Chunk boundaries determine which units the retriever can return. A small chunk may omit a needed condition; a large one may mix unrelated topics. Inspect missed evidence and compare chunking choices rather than assuming size alone explains a poor answer.

Hybrid retrieval offers two routes into the candidate set: dense similarity and lexical matching. RRF avoids adding incompatible raw score scales, but list depth, rank constant and filtering still need evaluation.

A cross-encoder scores each (query, chunk) pair jointly, permitting token interactions absent from a single-vector comparison. Measure whether that changes the ranking usefully and choose the shortlist size against the added scoring cost.

Prompt assembly determines chunk formatting, citation IDs, instructions, and evidence order. Some tested models answer more reliably when evidence is near the start or end, so vary its position during evaluation.

A passage can be present while the generated answer is wrong. Test different context lengths and positions. More passages can improve coverage or introduce distractors; the useful count is task-dependent.

Keep prompt assembly in a separate function and inspect the exact input. The instructions, question and source passages should have distinct roles. Check that source identifiers survive assembly and that the reader can trace an answer claim back to its supporting passage.

Give chunks traceable IDs tied to a source version. An ID-existence check catches nonexistent citations; it does not establish that the cited text supports the claim. Evaluate that relationship separately.

Advanced patterns

Some questions benefit from changing the search text or running another search based on the first result.

HyDE: embed the answer, not the question

HyDE generates a hypothetical answer passage, embeds it, and uses that vector to search for source passages.

Authored rankings illustrate a possible HyDE improvement. The hypothetical answer is a search aid, not evidence; real retrieved passages must support the final answer.

A generated hypothetical passage can supply useful vocabulary or answer structure for retrieval. An asymmetric query–passage encoder already learns to bridge question and document forms, so HyDE is an additional strategy to evaluate rather than a necessary correction.

The hypothetical passage may contain false details. Discard it as evidence and retrieve actual source passages.

HyDE adds generation work and can steer retrieval in the wrong direction when the guess is poor. Compare it with direct retrieval and query rewriting on your task.

Query rewriting

Another option: have the model rewrite the query before it goes into the retriever. Expand abbreviations, resolve pronouns, split compound questions, add synonyms. Useful when your users type things like "what about the other one" and expect the system to have some memory of the conversation.

Multi-hop RAG

Some questions require evidence spread across passages. In a fictional corpus, “which department does the designer of Orion lead?” needs the designer’s name and then that person’s department. One retrieval pass might find both; multiple passes can help when it does not.

Generate a sub-query, retrieve, inspect the result and decide whether another search is needed. Preserve intermediate evidence and stop on missing support, a useful answer, or a request budget. More hops can compound errors.

Agentic RAG: the model decides

Naive RAG retrieves on every turn. That's fine when every user message is a question about your corpus. It's wasteful, and sometimes harmful, when the message is "write me a haiku" or "what is 17 times 23." An unnecessary retrieval can add irrelevant material, latency and cost.

Retrieval becomes a tool the model chooses to call, the same way it would choose to call a calculator or a code interpreter.

The gate can be an application rule, a separate classifier or a model tool call. Self-RAG trains a model with reflection tokens that control retrieval and critique evidence and generated text. It is a specific learned system, not simply thresholding any model’s self-reported confidence.

Self-RAG’s relevance, support and usefulness judgments participate in generation and selection. They are model predictions, so they can be wrong; reflection tokens are not external factual verification.

Optional retrieval can avoid irrelevant searches, but it can also skip a needed one. Include both types of routing error in the evaluation.

Here kkk is the RRF rank constant, often set to 60; it is separate from the number of returned neighbors. A chunk absent from a returned list contributes zero for that list.

Evaluation: the triad

A RAG system has at least three places where it can be wrong, and they need to be measured separately. Treating "did the answer look right" as the metric hides which stage is actually failing.

RAGAS provides automated estimators for several dimensions. Its original faithfulness, answer relevance and context relevance metrics should not be confused with reference-answer correctness.

Retrieval quality. Did the candidate set include the needed evidence, and how prominently? Precision, recall and rank-sensitive measures answer different questions. Define the unit being judged: documents, passages or claims.

Faithfulness. Are the answer’s claims supported by the provided context? A faithful answer can still repeat a false or obsolete source. Automated entailment judgments require validation.

Answer relevance and correctness. Does the response address the question, and is it right under an authoritative reference? Those are separate properties; a relevant answer can be incorrect.

Inspect disagreements between these measures instead of inferring a specific failure from one score. The same aggregate number can arise from several different error patterns.

Check corpus coverage as well as retrieval quality: does the source collection contain the evidence needed for the expected questions? Missing source material cannot be recovered by tuning the retriever.

Build a held-out set with answer and evidence labels. Log candidates, the assembled prompt and the final answer for each question so that a failed score can be traced to the text that produced it.

Misconceptions

"RAG is always a preprocessing step." A fixed retrieve-then-generate pipeline is one design. Retrieval can also be a tool invoked repeatedly during a task.

"Every hallucination is a retrieval problem." Missing evidence, contradictory evidence and unsupported generation are different problems. Inspect the actual context before assigning blame.

"More retrieved chunks is always better." More chunks can supply missing evidence, but also introduce distractors, contradictions, and additional input cost. Choose the context budget using held-out questions and evidence-coverage checks.

"Improving embeddings fixes retrieval failures." Embedding quality matters alongside chunking, candidate retrieval, reranking and answer generation. Inspect failures before deciding which stage to improve.

"A longer context window makes RAG obsolete." Longer inputs change the tradeoff. Including the full corpus can avoid retrieval misses when it fits; retrieval can reduce input work and exclude irrelevant material. Compare answer quality, total latency and cost on the same questions, including the effect of caching. The context-window post examines evidence for both approaches.

What's next

Some questions need evidence from several passages: themes across a corpus, or relationships connecting people and projects. A flat retriever can succeed if it returns all the needed evidence. Graph-based retrieval offers another way to find and organize those connections.

The next post covers GraphRAG and knowledge graphs, where we do extra work at ingest time (extract entities, build a graph, detect communities, pre-summarize) to support questions about relationships and themes across documents.

Additional reading (and watching)

  • Chen, T., et al. (2024). Dense X Retrieval. Studies retrieval granularity.

  • Nogueira, R. & Cho, K. (2019). Passage Re-ranking with BERT. arXiv:1901.04085. The foundational paper for BERT-based cross-encoder reranking in IR.

  • Liu, N. F., et al. (2023). Lost in the Middle. Measures task performance as relevant information changes position, not attention weights.

  • Gao, L., et al. (2022). Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels. arXiv:2212.10496. The HyDE paper: embed a hallucinated answer, retrieve against it.

  • Lewis, P., et al. (2020). Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks. NeurIPS 2020. The original RAG paper, introducing the retrieve-then-generate architecture end-to-end with a trainable retriever.

  • Asai, A., et al. (2023). Self-RAG: Learning to Retrieve, Generate, and Critique through Self-Reflection. ICLR 2024. Introduces retrieve/critique tokens so the model controls its own retrieval and evaluates its own outputs.

  • Es, S., et al. (2023). RAGAS. Reference-free evaluation of faithfulness, answer relevance and context relevance.

  • Anthropic. (2024). Introducing Contextual Retrieval. Engineering post (September 2024). Evaluates adding generated source context to chunks before embedding and lexical indexing.

  • Guu, K., et al. (2020). REALM: Retrieval-Augmented Language Model Pre-Training. ICML 2020. Shows how a retriever can be trained jointly with the LM via masked-LM signal; predecessor to RAG.

  • Borgeaud, S., et al. (2022). Improving Language Models by Retrieving from Trillions of Tokens. ICML 2022. DeepMind's RETRO; architectural retrieval via cross-attention to neighbors from a massive text database.

  • Trivedi, H., et al. (2023). Interleaving Retrieval with Chain-of-Thought Reasoning for Knowledge-Intensive Multi-Step Questions. ACL 2023. The "IRCoT" paper on multi-hop retrieval interleaved with reasoning steps.
