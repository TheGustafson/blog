52:["$","h2",null,{"children":"Misconceptions"}]
53:["$","p",null,{"children":[["$","strong",null,{"children":"\"Longer advertised context equals longer effective context.\""}]," Almost never. A model advertised at 128k might have effective usable context closer to 32k depending on how it was trained and what you're trying to do. Retrieval tasks like needle-in-a-haystack are the most forgiving, because finding a single fact only requires one good attention lookup. Reasoning tasks that need to combine multiple pieces of context fall apart much earlier. The RULER benchmark[^8] and LongBench[^9] exist because advertised-vs-effective is a real gap, and you should never trust a context-length claim without seeing actual task numbers."]}]
54:["$","p",null,{"children":[["$","strong",null,{"children":"\"You need to retrain from scratch to get long context.\""}]," You don't. The whole point of YaRN and its cousins is that you keep the weights from the short-context pretraining and add an extension on top. The fine-tuning budget for 128k is often well under 1% of the pretraining budget. This is what makes 128k and 1M context windows economically possible."]}]
55:["$","p",null,{"children":[["$","strong",null,{"children":"\"Attention's quadratic cost is the real bottleneck.\""}]," For training, yes, which is exactly why progressive extension matters. Training at 128k costs 1024× more attention FLOPs per token than training at 4k, so you'd never want to do it for very long. A 128k-token KV cache for a 70B model is tens of gigabytes of data that has to be read every decode step. Long-context inference is a different engineering problem than long-context training, and the ",["$","a",null,{"href":"/blog/paged-attention","children":"PagedAttention"}]," and ",["$","a",null,{"href":"/blog/prefill-vs-decode","children":"prefill-vs-decode"}]," posts are where that story lives."]}]
