Arc 8: Evaluation & Scientific Discipline

Evaluating Reasoning

8 min readbuilds on Contamination and Leakage, Eval Harnesses: lm-eval-harness and HELM, The RLHF Pipeline: Reward Models and PPO and Sampling Strategies: Temperature, Top-k, Top-p, and Min-p

A note on this series

I built this series as a learning project and used Claude extensively for research, drafting, critique, coding, and revision. I chose the curriculum and dependency order, drove the questions, edited the explanations, developed the interactive examples, and take responsibility for the published result.

This is an educational first edition rather than a formal reference. I am reviewing the articles against primary sources, testing the executable examples, and tracking corrections as the series matures. Some examples require specific hardware, model weights, or external services; they are teaching examples, not a tested deployment recipe.

A correct answer is useful evidence, but it leaves a question open: does the model succeed when the problem changes in a way that requires the same underlying method? One answer could have been recalled, guessed, or reached through invalid work that happened to end in the right place.

Reasoning evaluations approach that question through sets of problems, controlled variations, executable checks, and sometimes judgments of intermediate work. None gives a direct view of a model’s internal computation.

This follows contamination and eval harnesses. Those concerns still apply: hard questions can leak, and a parser or inference budget can change a reported result.

Multiple choice can still be difficult

Four answer choices provide information that an open response does not. Uniform random guessing gives 25% expected accuracy, and eliminating distractors can improve that rate. That is a baseline, not a floor: a model can systematically select wrong options and score below it.

But multiple-choice format does not make a task merely a test of recall. GPQA contains expert-written questions in biology, chemistry, and physics, with four answer options. Its difficulty comes from the content and distractors, not from asking the model to generate an unrestricted answer.

The original GPQA study reported 65% accuracy for domain experts, rising to 74% after discounting clear mistakes identified retrospectively. Skilled nonexperts averaged 34% despite access to the web. Use these numbers as comparisons within the original study’s human protocols.

Open-ended output removes the supplied candidates but introduces other choices: acceptable equivalent answers, numeric tolerance, parsing, and how to grade explanations. It can also be memorized.

What a score means mathematically

For a fixed evaluation set with answer keys yiy_iyi​, an answer-only accuracy is

A^=1n∑i=1n1[judge⁡(y^i,yi)=correct].\widehat{A}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}[\operatorname{judge}(\hat y_i,y_i)=\text{correct}].A=n1​i=1∑n​1[judge(y^​i​,yi​)=correct].

The judge may be an exact matcher, an execution test, or another evaluator. This expression records its decisions; it does not identify whether a successful answer came from recall, a learned algorithm, or a mixture.

A toy mixture makes the ambiguity explicit. If a system solves a fraction rrr of questions and guesses uniformly among four options on the rest, its expected accuracy is r+(1−r)/4r+(1-r)/4r+(1−r)/4. That formula follows from the assumed two-path process. An observed accuracy does not prove that a real model actually uses those paths.

Even probabilities over choices need care. If you normalize likelihoods over the four offered candidates, the result is a distribution conditional on those candidates. It is not necessarily the probability that unconstrained generation would produce each answer, and it is not automatically calibrated to correctness.

Choosing tasks and reporting versions

FrontierMath uses expert-written advanced mathematics problems with answers designed for automatic checking. That supports reproducible outcome evaluation, but an accepted final answer does not certify every step of a supplied proof. Report the benchmark version and subset; performance claims age as models and evaluation protocols change.

ARC-AGI asks a system to infer transformations from examples and produce an output grid. Different benchmark generations and public or private splits are distinct evaluations. The allowed inference budget, number of attempts, and access to public tasks matter. Public grid tasks can be memorized or used for development like other public data.

Fresh tasks, controlled variations, and held-out problem families can strengthen the evidence for transfer. They still need quality checks. A rewritten question can accidentally change its answer, introduce ambiguity, or alter difficulty in ways unrelated to the ability being tested.

Inspecting the written work

A final-answer checker can accept a solution containing an invalid derivation. Conversely, a model can produce useful valid steps and then make an arithmetic error at the end. Step-level annotation makes those failures visible.

Unlike outcome-level reward models trained on preferences or final answers across whole responses, a process reward model learns to score intermediate steps t∈{1,…,T}t \in \{1,\dots,T\}t∈{1,…,T} from step-level supervision. An outcome reward model learns from labels on complete solutions. An outcome model may still read the entire trace; what differs is the supervision signal, not necessarily which text it can access.

A step scorer also makes mistakes. It may accept a plausible false claim, miss a dependency on an earlier error, or react to superficial formatting. Evaluate it against held-out annotations and failure cases. A readable chain of thought can be checked as text without assuming it faithfully reports the internal computation that produced the answer.

Test-time computation is part of the system

Drawing multiple candidates with stochastic sampling strategies and selecting one can improve results under some conditions. Selection might use majority voting, an executable verifier, an outcome scorer, or a process scorer.

When an executable verifier is available, drawing n≥kn \ge kn≥k independent samples and counting the ccc correct solutions yields Chen et al.’s unbiased estimator for pass@k\text{pass@}kpass@k:

pass@k^=1−(n−ck)(nk).\widehat{\text{pass@}k}=1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}.pass@k​=1−(kn​)(kn−c​)​.

Report the candidate budget and selection procedure alongside the metric. Because pass@k\text{pass@}kpass@k asks whether at least one of kkk candidates passed, it assumes a verifier or oracle can identify that candidate; it differs from the accuracy of a single answer selected by majority vote or a learned reward model without test execution. Also track tokens, tool calls, elapsed time, and any task-specific retries needed to obtain the result.

When a test has little headroom

A high-scoring benchmark can still expose important failures and regressions. As the number of remaining errors shrinks, however, a small test has less resolution for comparing improvements. Repeated optimization against it can also narrow what the score represents.

Use paired item-level comparisons and keep regression tests. Add independently held-out tasks when you need evidence about abilities outside the old test’s coverage. A new hard benchmark is useful if its construction and scoring support the intended claim, not simply because the first published scores are low.

The tool-agent evaluation post extends this concern to systems whose answers include actions and changes to an environment.
