Arc 7: Training & Post-Training

LoRA and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

14 min readbuilds on Supervised Fine-Tuning, Vectors, Matrices, and the Spaces They Live In and GPUs, Floating Point, and Why Precision Matters

A note on this series

I built this series as a learning project and used Claude extensively for research, drafting, critique, coding, and revision. I chose the curriculum and dependency order, drove the questions, edited the explanations, developed the interactive examples, and take responsibility for the published result.

This is an educational first edition rather than a formal reference. I am reviewing the articles against primary sources, testing the executable examples, and tracking corrections as the series matures. Some examples require specific hardware, model weights, or external services; they are teaching examples, not a tested deployment recipe.

Under the mixed-precision Adam accounting used below, 70 billion trainable parameters need 1.12 TB of model state before activations. That is the combined nominal capacity of fourteen 80 GB GPUs, with no room yet for temporary buffers or computation.

A fine-tune may need to change behavior without updating every parameter. LoRA trains small matrices alongside frozen weights, reducing trainable state and making task-specific checkpoints much smaller.

Prerequisites

This post assumes you've read:

  • Supervised Fine-Tuning for what "fine-tuning" actually means (and how it differs from pretraining).
  • Vectors, Matrices, and the Spaces They Live In for the notion of a matrix rank and what a linear map is doing.
  • GPUs, Floating Point, and Why Precision Matters for fp16 vs fp32 and quantization basics (needed for QLoRA).

The full fine-tuning bill

For each trainable parameter during mixed-precision training with Adam, you typically carry:

  • The weight itself in fp16 (2 bytes).
  • Its gradient in fp16 (2 bytes).
  • Two optimizer moments in fp32: mmm and vvv (8 bytes combined).
  • A master copy of the weight in fp32 for the optimizer update (4 bytes).

That's roughly 16 bytes per trainable parameter, sometimes called the "Adam tax." For a 70B model, you're looking at:

70,000,000,000×16 bytes≈1.1 TB70{,}000{,}000{,}000 \times 16 \text{ bytes} \approx 1.1 \text{ TB}70,000,000,000×16 bytes≈1.1 TB

This 16-byte accounting assumes a particular mixed-precision Adam implementation. Sharding distributes this state across devices; offloading moves it to other memory. Activation checkpointing reduces activation storage, which is not included in the 16 bytes.

Five separate 70B checkpoints require about 700 GB of 16-bit weight storage. Keeping every copy resident also requires that weight capacity across devices, plus serving overhead. Sharing a base and storing smaller task-specific updates can reduce the duplication.

Restricting the update

Aghajanyan et al. found that some language-model fine-tuning tasks could be learned in a low-dimensional parameter subspace. That motivates restricting updates, but it does not prove that each layer’s update matrix has low rank.

LoRA makes that additional modeling choice: represent an update as the product of two smaller matrices and test whether the constraint is sufficient for the task.

The math, stripped down

Take any linear layer in the model:

y=Wx,W∈Rd×dy = W x, \quad W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}y=Wx,W∈Rd×d

In full fine-tuning, we let WWW move around freely. The update is ΔW\Delta WΔW, also d×dd \times dd×d. That's d2d^2d2 trainable parameters per linear layer.

LoRA does this instead:

W′=W+ΔW,ΔW=BAW' = W + \Delta W, \quad \Delta W = B AW′=W+ΔW,ΔW=BA

where B∈Rd×rB \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}B∈Rd×r and A∈Rr×dA \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}A∈Rr×d with r≪dr \ll dr≪d.

WWW is frozen, so it needs neither parameter gradients nor Adam states.

We only train BBB and AAA. Together they have 2⋅r⋅d2 \cdot r \cdot d2⋅r⋅d parameters. When we multiply them, we get a matrix with the same shape as WWW, but whose rank is at most rrr. That's the thing we add to WWW to form the effective weight.

For a Llama-7B-style Linear layer with d=4096d = 4096d=4096:

  • Full FT: d2=16,777,216d^2 = 16{,}777{,}216d2=16,777,216 trainable params.
  • LoRA, r=8r = 8r=8: 2rd=65,5362rd = 65{,}5362rd=65,536 trainable params.
  • Ratio: 0.39%.

Change the rank to compare the frozen base W with the trainable factors B and A. At d=4,096 and r=8, the factors contain about 65K parameters, versus 16M in the full layer: roughly 0.4%.

LoRA restricts the update’s rank, while full fine-tuning does not. The 0.39% figure counts trainable parameters in this layer; it is not the fraction of training compute. Forward and backward work through the base model still costs time and memory.

What changes inside the forward pass

The LoRA forward pass combines the frozen base layer with the trainable adapter branch.

At training time, given input xxx:

y=Wx+BAxy = W x + B A xy=Wx+BAx

You compute two things in parallel: the original WxWxWx (using the frozen weight) and the LoRA branch BAxBAxBAx (using the trainable pair). You add them. With the efficient association B(Ax)B(Ax)B(Ax), this is three matrix multiplications: WxWxWx, AxAxAx, and B(Ax)B(Ax)B(Ax). The two added multiplications involve the small rank rrr.

The optimizer does not need a gradient or Adam states for the frozen weight WWW. Gradients with respect to its input can still pass through WxWxWx to earlier trainable adapters. Freezing parameters is not the same as detaching the whole branch.

LoRA also multiplies the adapter by α/r\alpha/rα/r. This exposes a scale to tune alongside the rank, target modules, and learning rate.

So the full forward pass is:

y=Wx+αr⋅BAxy = W x + \frac{\alpha}{r} \cdot B A xy=Wx+rα​⋅BAx

At inference time, separate adapters allow one frozen base to support different updates. Alternatively, merge Wmerged=W+(α/r)BAW_{\text{merged}} = W + (\alpha/r)BAWmerged​=W+(α/r)BA and use one dense matmul.

Worked example

Say we have a Llama-2-7B model and we want to fine-tune it on internal company documentation.

7B model dimensions:

  • Hidden size d=4096d = 4096d=4096.
  • Number of transformer layers: 32.
  • Attention projections: WQW_QWQ​, WKW_KWK​, WVW_VWV​, WOW_OWO​, each d×dd \times dd×d.
  • FFN: standard SwiGLU has three projections of size around d×(d⋅ff_mult)d \times (d \cdot \text{ff\_mult})d×(d⋅ff_mult).

In the original LoRA paper, they apply adapters to WQW_QWQ​ and WVW_VWV​ only. That's 2 Linear layers per block × 32 blocks = 64 Linear layers being LoRA-ized.

With r=8r = 8r=8, each adapter adds:

2⋅r⋅d=2⋅8⋅4096=65,536 params2 \cdot r \cdot d = 2 \cdot 8 \cdot 4096 = 65{,}536 \text{ params}2⋅r⋅d=2⋅8⋅4096=65,536 params

Across 64 adapters: 64×65,536≈4.264 \times 65{,}536 \approx 4.264×65,536≈4.2 million trainable parameters. The base model has about 6.7 billion parameters. So we're training 0.06% of the model.

For this example, frozen 16-bit base weights occupy about 13.4 GB. The 4.2M adapter parameters require about 67 MB under the earlier 16-byte training-state accounting. These are decimal units.

Activations, temporary buffers, and implementation overhead are additional. Their size depends on sequence length, batch size, checkpointing, attention kernels, and dtype; these parameter counts alone do not establish that a specific GPU can run the fine-tune.

Picking the rank

Rank limits the update matrix, and the target-module choice determines where those updates are allowed. A small rank may suffice for one task and underfit another. Sweep rank on a fixed evaluation set rather than treating 8 or 16 as universal thresholds.

At r≥dr\geq dr≥d, a square layer can represent a full-rank update, so LoRA does not have an accuracy ceiling that persists at every possible rank. At that point its parameter-efficiency advantage may disappear.

QLoRA: quantizing the frozen base

LoRA reduces trainable state but leaves frozen base weights to store. A 70B base in 16-bit precision occupies 140 GB before other allocations.

QLoRA, from Dettmers et al., combines several memory-saving choices:

  1. Quantize the base model to 4-bit using a format called NF4 (NormalFloat 4, which spaces values to match a Gaussian distribution rather than uniformly). This takes the 140 GB frozen base down to about 35 GB.
  2. Keep the LoRA adapters trainable in higher precision. The adapters occupy a small share of the state and retain higher precision for training.
  3. Double-quantize the quantization constants. The NF4 codebook has per-block scales. Quantizing those scales reduces their storage too.
  4. Page optimizer states to CPU when they don't fit in GPU memory, using NVIDIA's unified memory feature. The benefit depends on adapter size and peak allocations; paging also has transfer costs.

QLoRA dequantizes base weights to a compute dtype such as bfloat16 for operations while retaining their compressed storage. Whether dequantization is fused into a matrix multiplication depends on the kernel and training implementation.

The QLoRA paper reports fine-tuning a 65B model on one 48 GB GPU and competitive results in its evaluated settings. That demonstrates a working memory-saving recipe, not a guarantee that any 65B model, sequence length, or batch fits in 48 GB without a quality change.

Merging, and what it buys you

Once training is done, you have a choice. You can keep the adapter separate and compose it with the base at inference time (as y=Wx+(α/r)BAxy = Wx + (\alpha/r)BAxy=Wx+(α/r)BAx). Or you can precompute W′=W+(α/r)BAW' = W + (\alpha/r)BAW′=W+(α/r)BA and replace WWW in the model outright.

In exact arithmetic, merging gives the same linear map. Floating-point rounding and requantization can introduce differences. A merged dense layer removes the adapter’s extra operations; measured end-to-end latency still depends on serving details.

The separate form has two wins:

  1. One base model, many tasks. You keep one copy of WWW resident, and you swap a small adapter (a few MB each) per request. Serving systems can batch or switch adapters while reusing the base.
  2. Smaller transfers. Switching a resident or cached adapter can avoid loading a full base checkpoint. Transfer time depends on adapter size, storage, memory residency, and the serving system.

The broader PEFT family

Other parameter-efficient methods place trainable parameters in different parts of the model:

  • BitFit trains selected bias parameters. Its trainable fraction depends on the architecture.
  • Prompt tuning learns embeddings prepended at the input. Prefix tuning learns attention key/value prefixes across layers; these are different parameterizations.
  • Adapters insert trainable bottleneck modules into a frozen network, adding operations that generally cannot be absorbed into one existing linear weight.
  • LoRA trains an additive low-rank update.
  • DoRA separates magnitude and direction, using a low-rank direction update plus trainable magnitude. Compare it empirically with LoRA for the chosen setup.
  • QLoRA combines a quantized frozen base with higher-precision adapter training.

Choose the method against a memory budget and held-out task performance. Parameter count alone does not predict latency or quality.

Implementation sketch

This wrapper adds trainable factors AAA and BBB to a frozen linear layer.

Zero-initializing BBB makes the initial adapter update zero. This preserves the base layer at initialization. Computing B(Ax)B(Ax)B(Ax) avoids materializing a dense update on every forward pass. The wrapper preserves the base layer’s dtype and device and assumes an ordinary floating-point nn.Linear, not a quantized module.

Real libraries (Hugging Face's PEFT, LoRAX, FSDP-LoRA) have more machinery for state-dict handling, quantized-base support, target-module selection, and multi-adapter loading.

Misconceptions

"LoRA must recover full fine-tuning." A fixed-rank adapter may not represent the update a task needs, and optimization may find a different solution even when capacity is sufficient. Rank, target modules, data, and training settings all affect the result.

"More rank is always better." More rank permits more updates and consumes more trainable state. Whether it improves held-out quality needs measurement; learning rate and target-module selection can matter as much as rank.

"Any 4-bit scheme gives the same result as QLoRA." QLoRA combines NF4, double quantization, and paged optimizers in a particular recipe. Other quantizers and kernels can have different memory, speed, and quality trade-offs.

"You can’t merge an adapter trained with QLoRA." Merging into a higher-precision base is possible. Keeping that merged checkpoint in BF16 loses the base’s 4-bit storage saving; requantizing it can restore compressed storage but changes the numerical result. Serving the quantized base with a separate adapter is another option.

Additional reading (and watching)

  • Rajbhandari, S., et al. (2020). ZeRO: Memory Optimizations Toward Training Trillion Parameter Models. SC 2020. The paper that put clear per-parameter byte counts on Adam-style training and introduced the sharding strategies and ideal model-state memory accounting.

  • Hu, E. J., et al. (2021). LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models. ICLR 2022. The canonical reference. Defines the W+BAW + BAW+BA update, the α/r\alpha / rα/r scaling, the merge trick, and the "adapt only Q and V" recommendation.

  • Kingma, D. & Ba, J. (2015). Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization. ICLR 2015. Source of the mmm and vvv state that makes fine-tuning so memory-hungry in the first place.

  • Aghajanyan, A., et al. (2020). Intrinsic Dimensionality Explains the Effectiveness of Language Model Fine-Tuning. ACL 2021. The paper that made "the update lives in a low-dimensional subspace" quantitative, which set up LoRA's existence.

  • Lialin, V., et al. (2023). Scaling Down to Scale Up: A Guide to Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning. A survey for the full PEFT landscape, including rank-vs-quality findings across many methods.

  • Dettmers, T., et al. (2023). QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs. NeurIPS 2023. Source of NF4, double quantization, paged optimizer offload, and the 65B-on-a-single-48GB-GPU result.

  • Sheng, Y., et al. (2023). S-LoRA: Serving Thousands of Concurrent LoRA Adapters. MLSys 2024. Studies shared-base serving, adapter memory management, and batching under specified workloads.

  • Ben Zaken, E., Goldberg, Y., & Ravfogel, S. (2021). BitFit: Simple Parameter-efficient Fine-tuning for Transformer-based Masked Language-models. ACL 2022. Train only the biases; a strong minimalist baseline in the PEFT family.

  • Pfeiffer, J., et al. (2020). AdapterHub: A Framework for Adapting Transformers. EMNLP 2020 (System Demos). The adapters-as-insert-modules precursor to LoRA.

  • Liu, S.-Y., et al. (2024). DoRA: Weight-Decomposed Low-Rank Adaptation. ICML 2024. Splits WWW into magnitude and direction, applies LoRA to the direction only. Reports comparisons with LoRA in the paper’s evaluated settings.

  • Mangrulkar, S., et al. (2022). PEFT: State-of-the-Art Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning Methods. GitHub. The reference library for applying LoRA and friends to Hugging Face models.

  • Ryu, S. (2023). Low-rank Adaptation for Fast Text-to-Image Diffusion Fine-tuning. GitHub. The port of LoRA to Stable Diffusion that kicked off the SD LoRA ecosystem.

  • Lialin, V., et al. (2023). ReLoRA: High-Rank Training Through Low-Rank Updates. NeurIPS 2023 workshop. Stacks LoRA updates over training so the effective rank grows, bridging the gap between LoRA and full FT.
