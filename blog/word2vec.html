Arc 2: Language Modeling Before Transformers

Word2vec and the Embedding Revolution

16 min read

How do you tell a computer that "cat" and "kitten" are related, but "cat" and "economics" aren't?

For a long time, the answer was: you don't. Words were one-hot vectors. If your vocabulary has 50,000 words, "cat" is a vector with a 1 in position 7,243 and zeros everywhere else. "Kitten" is a 1 in position 12,891. The dot product between them is zero, same as the dot product between "cat" and "economics." Every word is equally distant from every other word. No notion of similarity is baked into the representation.

This is a problem if you're trying to build language models, because language is built on similarity. You know what a sentence means partly because you know which words could substitute for which. So what you really want is a representation where similar words live nearby in some continuous space, and dissimilar words live far apart. You want a dense vector, maybe 300 dimensions, where the position of the word carries information about what it means.

In 2013, Tomas Mikolov and his team at Google published a paper that changed the field.[^1] The method was called word2vec, and the core idea was simple.

The distributional hypothesis

There's a line from the linguist J.R. Firth, written in 1957, that became the entire theoretical backbone of word embeddings: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps."

The distributional hypothesis says that words appearing in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. "Cat" and "dog" both show up near words like "pet," "fur," "veterinarian." "King" and "queen" both show up near "throne," "crown," "royal." If you could somehow compress a word's typical context into a fixed-size vector, words with similar contexts would end up with similar vectors.

That's the entire game. Word2vec is a clever way of learning those context-based vectors.

Before we get into how it learns them, here's what the finished product feels like. In a well-trained embedding space, cosine similarity between vectors ranks words by meaning. The top match for "cat" is "kitten", then "dog", then "tiger". The top match for "car" is "truck", then "bike". Skip-gram: predict context from a word

The skip-gram model works like this. You slide a window across your training corpus. In the center of the window is your center word, and the words around it (within some radius, say 2 words on each side) are the context words. The model's job is: given the center word, predict the context words.

Say your sentence is "the cat sat on the mat" and your window size is 2. When the center word is "sat," the training pairs are:

| Center word | Context word | |-------------|-------------| | sat | the | | sat | cat | | sat | on | | sat | the |

You generate these pairs for every position in the corpus. Millions of them.

A radius-2 window slides across the sentence. At each position, the blue center word emits up to 4 training pairs, one per context token in the window. Over a real corpus this is billions of pairs, each one a tiny piece of evidence about which words keep company with which.

The architecture is barely a neural network. It's a single hidden layer with no activation function. The input is a one-hot vector for the center word. That gets multiplied by a weight matrix W∈RV×dW \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}W∈RV×d, where VVV is the vocabulary size and ddd is the embedding dimension (typically 100-300). This multiplication is a lookup in disguise: pulling out the row of WWW corresponding to the center word. That row is the word's embedding.

And here's the key insight: we don't actually care about the predictions. We care about the weight matrix WWW. After training, the rows of WWW are the word embeddings. The prediction task is just a vehicle for learning useful representations.

CBOW: the mirror image

There's a second variant called Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW) that flips the direction. Instead of predicting context from a center word, CBOW predicts the center word from its context. You average the embeddings of all the context words and use that average to predict the word in the middle.

CBOW trains faster because each training example uses multiple context words simultaneously (one forward pass, one gradient update). Skip-gram is slower but tends to do better on rare words, because each rare word still generates several training pairs. In practice, most people used skip-gram. But the architecture barely matters compared to the next trick.

Negative sampling: making it practical

There's a computational problem with the skip-gram softmax. That denominator sums over the entire vocabulary. If you have 50,000 words, every single training step requires computing 50,000 dot products and exponentials just to normalize the probability. For millions of training pairs, this is brutal.

Mikolov's second paper introduced negative sampling as a solution.[^2] The intuition is straightforward: instead of computing a probability distribution over all 50,000 words, just train a binary classifier. For a real (center, context) pair, the model should output a high score. For a fake pair where the context word is a random word sampled from the vocabulary, the model should output a low score.

For each real training pair like (sat, cat), you sample maybe 5 random "negative" words (say "elephant," "quarterly," "Nebraska," "jumps," "the"). Now instead of updating weights for all 50,000 output neurons, you're only touching 6: the one correct word and 5 random wrong ones.

The negative sampling objective for a single (center, context) pair looks like:

where σ\sigmaσ is the sigmoid function and kkk is the number of negative samples (typically 5-20). The first term pushes the real pair's dot product up. The sum pushes the fake pairs' dot products down.

The bars are value-accurate: 1,000 dot products versus 6 on this toy vocab, and the ratio only stretches further at real V.

This took word2vec from an interesting idea to something you could actually train on a big corpus in hours on a single machine. Levy and Goldberg later showed that this objective implicitly factorizes a shifted pointwise mutual information matrix, which connects negative sampling back to classical distributional semantics.[^3] But in 2013, what mattered was that it worked, and it was fast.

The analogy property

And then something interesting fell out. When people started probing the trained embeddings, they found linear structure.

Take the vector for "king," subtract the vector for "man," add the vector for "woman," and the nearest neighbor to the result is "queen."

vking−vman+vwoman≈vqueenv_{\text{king}} - v_{\text{man}} + v_{\text{woman}} \approx v_{\text{queen}}vking​−vman​+vwoman​≈vqueen​

This suggested that the embedding space had learned a consistent "gender" direction, and a consistent "royalty" direction, and those directions composed linearly. The same trick worked for country-capital relationships (Paris - France + Italy ≈ Rome), verb tenses (walking - walked + swam ≈ swimming), and a bunch of other semantic relationships.

The training data generation is the tedious part: you need to slide your window across the corpus, extract (center, context) pairs, and sample negatives proportional to word frequency (raised to the 0.75 power, a trick Mikolov found worked well empirically). But the model itself is almost disappointingly simple.

Misconceptions

"Word2vec understands meaning." It doesn't. Word2vec learns distributional similarity, which correlates with meaning but isn't the same thing. The model has no grounding. It doesn't know that a cat is a small furry animal. It knows that "cat" appears in similar contexts to "dog" and "kitten." Useful, but calling it understanding is a stretch. The vectors encode co-occurrence statistics compressed through a neural bottleneck.

"The analogy trick works reliably." The king/queen example is famous precisely because it works so cleanly. In practice, analogy accuracy on benchmark datasets like the Google Analogy Test Set hovers around 60-75% depending on the embedding and the category.[^2] Syntactic analogies (walk:walked :: swim:swam) tend to work better than semantic ones. And the method is sensitive to how you measure "nearest neighbor" (cosine similarity vs. Euclidean distance matters). The analogy property is real and interesting, but it's more of a tendency than a law.

"You need huge datasets." The pretrained vectors Google released alongside the second word2vec paper were trained on a Google News corpus of about 100 billion words, which is part of where this assumption comes from. But you can get surprisingly useful embeddings from much smaller corpora, especially for domain-specific tasks. A few million words of medical text will give you medical embeddings where "aspirin" and "ibuprofen" cluster together even if "aspirin" and "ibuprofen" rarely appear in the same sentence.

These ideas in the wild (2026)

Word2vec was superseded technically years ago, but the core move (represent items as dense vectors learned from co-occurrence) is now everywhere. A few places it shows up today:

Every transformer embedding table. When you pass a token through GPT-5, Claude, or Llama, the very first thing that happens is the exact operation we just wrote: look up the token ID, pull out a row of a learned embedding matrix. The matrix is gigantic now (hundreds of thousands of rows, thousands of columns, jointly trained with the rest of the model instead of in isolation), but it's the same interface word2vec introduced. Every downstream layer receives those row vectors and refines them.[^7]

Dense retrieval and RAG. Semantic search over large document collections is done with sentence-level embeddings. Models like text-embedding-3-large, Cohere Embed v3, or the open-source BGE and Jina encoders dominate here. A user query gets embedded, then you ask a vector index for its nearest neighbors. That nearest-neighbor-in-vector-space query is the same cosine-similarity ranking you saw above, just scaled to billions of documents and 1,024+ dimensions.[^8]

Product and user embeddings in recommenders. Pinterest, Spotify, TikTok, and every ads platform you've interacted with train item embeddings using word2vec-descended objectives. The item being recommended plays the role of the "center word," and the items a user interacted with around it play the role of "context." The skip-gram-with-negative-sampling recipe is still standard for learning collaborative filtering embeddings, often under the name "item2vec" or "prod2vec."[^9]

What changed by 2026

Word2vec's biggest limitation is that each word gets exactly one vector. "Bank" has a single embedding regardless of whether you're talking about a river bank or a financial bank. That seems obvious now, but in 2013 it was considered an acceptable tradeoff, because getting any useful dense representation from raw text felt like a breakthrough.

The lineage from word2vec to modern LLMs is direct. Bengio's 2003 neural language model[^5] showed that learned word representations could improve language modeling. Collobert and Weston (2008) showed that a single set of learned embeddings could transfer across NLP tasks.[^6] Word2vec made the training fast enough and the embeddings good enough that the whole field reorganized around dense representations. GloVe and FastText extended the approach (next post). In 2017, the transformer arrived and replaced static word embeddings with contextual ones, where a word's representation depends on the sentence it appears in. But the embedding table at the bottom of every transformer is the direct descendant of word2vec's weight matrix WWW. The revolution was the idea that words should be dense vectors learned from context. Everything after is a refinement.
