Arc 12: Governance, Economics & Trajectory

A Short History of the Field: 2013–2026

19 min read

Most of this series has been about mechanisms. How a matmul works, how attention routes information, how a tokenizer splits a string, how a sampler picks the next word. Mechanisms are what the series is for. Intuitions about mechanisms are what lets the rest of the stack be legible.

This post is different. I want to walk through the field's last thirteen years chronologically, because the shape of today's stack is mostly downstream of a handful of specific moments, and most of those moments have had so much breathless coverage layered on top that the actual technical content has blurred. I find it useful, when something new lands in 2026, to be able to mentally locate it on a timeline next to the thing it's really a descendant of. That's what this post is trying to give you.

A warning up front: history written by people who lived through something is always a little off. I was using word2vec for production embeddings in 2016. I was an early Claude API customer. I remember exactly where I was when ChatGPT came out (in bed, on my phone, thinking "huh, InstructGPT with a chat wrapper, that's clever"). So the emphasis here reflects what I noticed. A real historian would weight things differently.

Why a history post in a technical series

The simplest answer: design decisions are downstream of incidents and scars. The transformer is shaped the way it is because RNNs hit a wall. RLHF is shaped the way it is because pure language modeling optima were weird to talk to. MCP exists because function calling schemas drifted between providers and nobody wanted to write seven adapters. Every current object in the stack has a reason it looks the way it does, and the reason is almost always "the previous thing didn't quite work."

If you don't see the previous thing, the current thing looks arbitrary. Once you see the previous thing, the current thing looks inevitable.

The other reason: there's a genre of "history of AI" post that mostly repeats marketing copy. I'd rather this one not be that. So next to each inflection, I'm going to try to say what I think actually changed, and what was coverage on top of a real but smaller shift.

The timeline

Autoplay, or click a year to jump. The rest of the post unpacks each era with more context.

2013: word2vec and the distributional revolution

Before 2013, "meaning" in NLP was a mess. You had WordNet with handcrafted synsets. You had latent semantic analysis, which was genuinely clever but computationally awkward. You had an enormous amount of feature engineering for anything downstream. The distributional hypothesis (you know a word by the company it keeps) had been around since Firth in the 1950s, but nobody had a clean, efficient way to turn it into usable vectors.

Mikolov and colleagues at Google published two papers that changed that. The algorithm was simple enough to implement in an afternoon. Run a shallow neural net that predicts context words from a target word (skip-gram) or a target word from context (CBOW), take the trained embedding table, throw away the rest. The famous analogy arithmetic (king − man + woman ≈ queen) fell out of this. That result isn't magic. It's what happens when you train a model whose objective is shaped like a dot product between related words. But in 2013 it felt like magic.

What was actually new: a cheap, stable way to produce dense word vectors at scale. What got marketed: semantic understanding. The vectors didn't understand anything. They just put co-occurring things near each other. That turned out to be enough for a lot of downstream tasks, and the pattern (pretrain a dense representation on an unsupervised objective, then use it for whatever) is the pattern the entire field has been running ever since.

GloVe and fastText refined the approach over the next year or two. By 2016, production systems at every major company had a word2vec-style embedding lookup somewhere.

2017: Attention Is All You Need

By 2017 the state of the art in sequence modeling was LSTM-based encoder-decoders with attention bolted on, and the state of the art was not great. Long-range dependencies were still a problem. Training was slow because recurrence forces sequential computation along the time axis, which doesn't parallelize across GPU cores.

Vaswani et al. at Google proposed a translation architecture that dropped recurrence entirely and used only attention and feedforward layers. The paper's original framing was narrow. Title aside, it was a Google paper (authors from Google Brain, Google Research, and the University of Toronto) about machine translation that beat LSTM baselines while being faster to train. The abstract mentions "quality, parallelization, and time to train." It doesn't contain the word "scaling."

The architecture itself was beautifully clean. Self-attention on the encoder side, masked self-attention plus cross-attention on the decoder side, a feedforward block after each attention block, residual connections everywhere, layer norm. The "Transformer block" that the rest of this series has been decomposing for you.

What was actually new: an architecture that parallelizes across tokens and scales cleanly on modern accelerators. What got marketed (eventually): "attention replaced recurrence." It did, but not overnight. LSTMs were still the default in most production NLP pipelines through 2018 and parts of 2019. People read the paper as a better translation model, full stop. The generality was latent, and it took Radford and OpenAI scaling the decoder stack to make it visible.

2018–2019: the transfer learning era

Two papers in 2018 cemented the recipe: pretrain a large transformer on a lot of text with a self-supervised objective, then finetune it on a small labeled dataset for whatever downstream task you care about.

GPT-1 (Radford et al., OpenAI, June 2018) used a decoder-only transformer trained to predict the next token. 117M parameters. BERT (Devlin et al., Google, October 2018) used an encoder-only transformer trained to fill in masked tokens. 340M parameters for BERT-Large. Both crushed the GLUE benchmark. Both made the previous approach (train a custom architecture from scratch on your labeled data) look silly in retrospect.

In February 2019 OpenAI published GPT-2. The title contains the framing that mattered: "Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners." The argument was that if you train a big enough language model on diverse enough text, it learns to do a lot of tasks without being told to, because all those tasks show up in its training data as ordinary text. Summarization appears in articles. Translation appears next to bilingual text. Question answering appears in QA forums. The model picks it up.

GPT-2 was also the first release where a lab staggered access, citing potential misuse. OpenAI released the smaller models first and the 1.5B-parameter full model later. That decision was controversial at the time. It set a template for how frontier labs would handle release decisions that we're still, uh, arguing about.

2020: GPT-3 and scaling laws

This is the moment the field reorganized itself around.

Two things happened in 2020 that together formed the scaling hypothesis. Kaplan et al. (OpenAI, January 2020) published "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models," showing that test loss on language modeling followed a clean power law in compute, parameters, and dataset size, across nearly eight orders of magnitude. Straight lines on log-log plots. The implication: if you want a better model, you don't need a better idea, you need more compute.

Four months later, Brown et al. published GPT-3. 175 billion parameters, 300 billion training tokens, and a demonstration that at this scale, few-shot in-context learning works. You can prompt the model with a handful of examples of a task and it will do the task, without any gradient updates. This was genuinely new. The scaling laws predicted that loss would go down. Nobody had predicted that specific capabilities would emerge as a phase transition.

Log-scale parameter counts for notable models. Each gridline is a 10x. The gap between word2vec and GPT-3 is roughly six orders of magnitude in six years.

What was actually new: few-shot prompting works at scale, and there's a clean empirical law connecting compute to loss. What got marketed: "general intelligence through scale." Not yet, but the framing stuck, and the entire next five years of capital allocation in the field can be read as a bet on that framing being more right than wrong.

The scaling laws also did something subtler. They turned model training from a research activity into a budgeting activity. If your loss-vs-compute curve is a straight line, you don't need to argue about architecture choices as much. You need a capital plan.

2022: RLHF and ChatGPT

Between GPT-3 in May 2020 and ChatGPT in November 2022 is the weirdest stretch in this history. The capability was available. Nobody outside the field noticed. GPT-3 was behind a waitlist, expensive, and a little annoying to use. Davinci would happily continue your prompt into nonsense if you weren't careful with formatting. It wasn't a product.

InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., OpenAI, March 2022) was the bridge. The recipe: take a pretrained language model, do supervised finetuning on human-written demonstrations, then do reinforcement learning against a reward model trained on human preference comparisons. The result is a model that follows instructions instead of continuing prompts. The difference in subjective usability is enormous. A raw language model is an autocomplete engine that happens to know a lot. An RLHF-tuned model is an assistant.

ChatGPT launched on November 30, 2022. It was InstructGPT plus a chat interface plus free access. The underlying model (GPT-3.5) was maybe six months old at launch. The chat wrapper was a weekend project compared to the work that went into the model itself. But the combination hit something in the distribution: a million users in five days, a hundred million in two months, the fastest consumer software adoption in history.

What was actually new: an existing technique (RLHF had been explored since at least 2017) had been applied to a large language model at enough scale that the product felt useful to people who weren't willing to learn prompt engineering. What got marketed: "the AI moment." It was the product moment. The model was old. That distinction matters because it tells you where capability gains actually come from: not from any single breakthrough, but from the accumulation of small things (pretraining, alignment finetuning, RLHF, chat UI, free access) composing into something a normal person can use.

2023: the open-weight explosion and the agent era

2023 is where the history gets hard to summarize because a lot happened and most of it was important.

GPT-4 shipped in March. Multimodal inputs, longer context, dramatically better reasoning. OpenAI didn't release the parameter count. The consensus estimate from leaks and inference benchmarking is that it's a mixture-of-experts model with a total of around 1.8 trillion parameters and roughly 280B active per forward pass, though nobody outside OpenAI actually knows. (A lesson we are, uh, still learning: the moment frontier labs stopped publishing architecture details, the research commons got noticeably thinner.)

Anthropic shipped Claude at roughly the same time. Google shipped Bard (later Gemini). The "frontier" became an oligopoly of maybe four labs.

And then Meta released LLaMA in February 2023. Weights leaked within a week. Meta leaned into the leak, subsequently releasing LLaMA 2 with an open license in July. Mistral (Jiang et al.) released a dense 7B model in September that beat LLaMA 2 13B. Suddenly every indie researcher, every Chinese lab, every startup could finetune a frontier-adjacent base model on their own data. The ratio of "papers per week" to "hours in a week" got silly.

Also in 2023, function calling landed as a structured API across providers. The idea was in the air (Toolformer had been published earlier in the year, ReAct prompting was from 2022), but OpenAI's June 2023 function-calling release made it a commodity. Every API now accepted a list of tool schemas, returned structured tool calls, and expected you to feed results back. The "agent loop" started working reliably enough to deploy.

The direction of travel over these two or three years was that the model started as a stateless function you called, acquired a loop around it, then a protocol around the loop, and by 2025 the protocols had started talking to each other.

2024: MoE at scale, long context, MCP

2024 was a consolidation year. Fewer paradigm shifts, more "the things we built last year actually work now."

Mixture-of-experts moved from a research curiosity to the way frontier labs build. Mixtral 8x7B (Mistral, December 2023) and especially Mixtral 8x22B (April 2024) made sparse MoE routing mainstream. DeepSeek V3 (December 2024) was the inflection point: 671 billion total parameters, 37 billion active per token, trained for something like $5.5M in compute, weights released openly. The open/closed gap, which had looked like it might keep widening forever, narrowed sharply.

Context windows crossed a million tokens. Gemini 1.5 Pro demonstrated million-token context in early 2024 with reasonable recall. Others followed. "Recall at the far end" remained mediocre across the board, which is the unsexy caveat that rarely makes the marketing.

Anthropic published the Model Context Protocol in November 2024. The pitch: tools for models should be a protocol, not a vendor-specific API. Any client can talk to any server. Any server can expose any tool. The initial uptake was slow. By mid-2025 it was the default, and by 2026 every IDE and every agent framework speaks MCP.

Computer use landed in 2024 too. Anthropic's computer use API let the model take screenshots and emit mouse/keyboard actions. OpenAI and others followed. The demo was impressive. Reliability in production remained, and in early 2026 still remains, a work in progress.

2025: test-time compute and agent protocols

The two big shifts of 2025 were both about spending compute differently.

Snell et al. (published late 2024) argued that test-time compute scales cleanly. If you let a model think longer, by generating longer chains of reasoning, by generating multiple candidates and voting, by searching over reasoning steps, its performance on hard problems goes up in a predictable way. OpenAI's o1 (September 2024) and o3 (announced December 2024, released April 2025) were the first frontier models explicitly designed around this axis. DeepSeek's R1 (January 2025) showed the same behavior could be elicited with pure reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards, and released the weights.

This changed the capital-allocation picture. Previously, "get a better model" meant "spend more on pretraining." Now it means "spend more on pretraining or spend more at inference." The frontier is two-dimensional.

Agent-to-agent protocols matured. Google published A2A in 2025. Remote MCP (MCP servers hosted as network services rather than local subprocesses) made delegation across trust boundaries tractable. By late 2025 it was normal for one agent to discover another agent's capabilities, negotiate a task, and hand work off.

Regulation arrived, at least on paper. The EU AI Act entered its phased implementation. Sector-specific rules in healthcare, finance, and education started landing in multiple jurisdictions. Whether any of this bit the frontier labs in a meaningful way is a question the "What's Next" post tries to answer honestly.

What was actually new vs what got marketed

Every one of these moments had a real kernel and a hype shell. Here they are side by side:

The left column is the thing that actually changed. The right column is the thing the press release said. Both are usually true, but at very different scales.

The pattern, if you look at the column on the left, is that the real advances are almost always architectural or empirical: a new efficient kernel, a clean power law, a protocol that removes friction between components. The column on the right tends to reach for nouns: "intelligence," "reasoning," "AGI sparks." The left-column items are what the next generation of systems will be built on. The right-column items are what ends up in the headlines.

The doubling time, roughly

Here's a small exponential fit to pin down how fast the parameter-count numbers actually grew. If we take word2vec (~0.1B, 2013) and extrapolate to the o1/R1 class (~1T, 2025), we have a factor of about 10410^4104 over twelve years. That's a doubling time of:

τdouble=12 yearslog⁡2(104)=1213.3≈0.9 years\tau_{\text{double}} = \frac{12 \text{ years}}{\log_2(10^4)} = \frac{12}{13.3} \approx 0.9 \text{ years}τdouble​=log2​(104)12 years​=13.312​≈0.9 years

So parameter count doubled roughly every ten or eleven months for most of this period. That's faster than Moore's law by a factor of two. It's also why the 2026 frontier looks nothing like the 2016 frontier, and why extrapolating another decade in either direction feels absurd in both directions.

The doubling has visibly slowed in 2025–2026, partly because MoE lets you grow total params without proportional compute, partly because test-time compute ate some of the pretraining budget, partly because GPUs got harder to procure. The exponential was always going to bend somewhere. It's bending now.

Misconceptions

"ChatGPT was a research breakthrough." It was a product breakthrough riding on a research program that was mostly already published. The model underneath ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) was derived from training runs that had been happening for more than a year. The RLHF alignment work (InstructGPT) had been published eight months earlier. The chat interface itself was a thin layer. What broke into the mainstream on November 30, 2022 was a new distribution, not a new capability. That distinction matters if you're trying to understand how technology diffusion actually works: rarely a single moment, usually an accumulation that crosses a usability threshold.

"Transformers replaced RNNs overnight." They didn't. The "Attention Is All You Need" paper shipped in June 2017. Production NLP systems at most companies were still LSTM-based through 2019. The replacement took two full years of "does this actually generalize beyond translation?" skepticism before the transfer-learning results from GPT-1 and BERT made the question moot. Paradigms shift slower than timelines suggest. Especially in industry codebases where somebody has to actually rewrite the training pipeline.

"Scaling laws guaranteed GPT-4." The scaling laws guaranteed that loss would keep going down. They did not guarantee that any specific capability (code generation, few-shot reasoning, tool use) would emerge at any specific parameter count. Those emergent jumps have been surprising at each scale, and in some cases, when you measure more carefully, the "emergence" turns out to be a smooth curve that looked jumpy because the benchmark was categorical. Scaling laws are a budgeting tool. They are not a capability roadmap.

"The agent era started in 2023." The ReAct paper is from late 2022. Toolformer was early 2023. Auto-GPT was a weekend project in March 2023 that caught fire because it was a vivid demo, not because it worked. Agents that reliably accomplish real tasks really did show up around 2024–2025, but the intellectual lineage (think-act-observe loops, language models deciding which tools to call) was already in place before function calling was a commodity API. The "era" starts wherever you draw the line, and the tempting lines are usually marked by product releases rather than by ideas.

Every paradigm took longer to become the production default than its launch coverage implied. The bar shows the gap between the paper and when most teams were actually using it. MCP is the outlier: protocol adoption compressed to about a year.

What's next

Thirteen years, word2vec to production agents over MCP. If you squint, the arc is: represent things as vectors, learn to predict next vectors, scale that until the vectors can do useful things, wrap the useful things in a protocol so they compose. The stack keeps getting taller, the abstraction keeps getting thicker, and the thing the practitioner does keeps moving up the stack.

The next (and final) post in the series is about what comes next: honest uncertainty about where the field goes from here, what I'd bet on, what I wouldn't, and what 2030 probably won't look like no matter what the keynotes say.

Additional reading (and watching)

  • Mikolov, T., Chen, K., Corrado, G., & Dean, J. (2013). Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space. The original word2vec paper; the companion negative-sampling paper shipped later that year.

  • Pennington, J., Socher, R., & Manning, C. D. (2014). GloVe: Global Vectors for Word Representation. EMNLP 2014. The other dominant word-embedding method of the mid-2010s.

  • Vaswani, A., et al. (2017). Attention Is All You Need. NeurIPS 2017. The transformer architecture, originally pitched for translation.

  • Radford, A., Narasimhan, K., Salimans, T., & Sutskever, I. (2018). Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training. The GPT-1 technical report.

  • Devlin, J., Chang, M.-W., Lee, K., & Toutanova, K. (2018). BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding. NAACL 2019. Masked-language-model pretraining that dominated NLP benchmarks for two years.

  • Radford, A., et al. (2019). Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners. The GPT-2 technical report. The phrase in the title is doing most of the work.

  • Kaplan, J., et al. (2020). Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models. The paper that turned model training into a budgeting activity.

  • Brown, T. B., et al. (2020). Language Models are Few-Shot Learners. NeurIPS 2020. GPT-3 and the few-shot prompting demonstration.

  • Ouyang, L., et al. (2022). Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback. NeurIPS 2022. The InstructGPT paper. ChatGPT is this plus a chat wrapper plus free access.

  • OpenAI. (2023). GPT-4 Technical Report. Sparse on architectural details, heavy on capability demonstrations.

  • Touvron, H., et al. (2023). LLaMA: Open and Efficient Foundation Language Models. The release that unlocked the open-weight ecosystem.

  • Jiang, A. Q., et al. (2023). Mistral 7B. A dense 7B that embarrassed much larger models on several benchmarks.

  • OpenAI. (2023). Function calling and other API updates. The announcement post that productized structured tool calls.

  • Jiang, A. Q., et al. (2024). Mixtral of Experts. Mistral's sparse MoE release.

  • DeepSeek-AI. (2024). DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report. 671B total, 37B active, open weights, ~$5.5M training run.

  • Anthropic. (2024). Introducing the Model Context Protocol. The MCP announcement.

  • Snell, C., et al. (2024). Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally can be More Effective than Scaling Model Parameters. The test-time compute scaling result.

  • DeepSeek-AI. (2025). DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning. Open reasoning-model weights with a clean RL recipe.

  • Google. (2025). Agent2Agent (A2A) Protocol. The cross-agent protocol announcement.

  • Schaeffer, R., Miranda, B., & Koyejo, S. (2023). Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?. NeurIPS 2023. Argues that many reported "emergent" capabilities are artifacts of discontinuous metrics.
