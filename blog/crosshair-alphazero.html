Crosshair: A New Board Game to Showcase Pure AlphaZero Self-Play

October 25, 20266 min read

In my previous post, Monte Carlo Tree Search is Awesome, we explored my favorite algorithm and made a bunch of different engines. One letdown though was that the AlphaZero-style Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe engine specifically had some issues: the learned value head got exploited by the search and lost 4–44 against plain random rollouts. I could probably spend some more time on the training and make it work, but I thought it might be more fun to design a new game entirely that is more amenable to AlphaZero-style training when you only have one 4060 Ti to train on.

So I created a game I'm calling Crosshair. It is a mashup of Hex and Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe on a 7×7 grid. Hex and Ultimate have some rules that make training from pure self-play a little harder (tho not impossible ofc, I just didn't power through it).

If you don't want to read all this and just want to play, click here, or if you just want to see the code, it's on GitHub and crates.io.

In Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe, you have 81 squares, long games, lots of draws that muddle the win/loss signal, and "sent to an already-won board" wildcards that suddenly blow the branching factor wide open. Hex has no draws, which is great, but on an 11×11 or 13×13 board you have over 100 legal moves every turn, so MCTS spreads a small simulation budget way too thin without hand-written bridge patterns or RAVE.

Crosshair takes the best parts of both and strips out the headaches. It is played on a compact 7×7 grid (49 squares). Red goes first and tries to connect the top and bottom edges with an orthogonal chain of stones (up, down, left, right, no diagonals). Blue tries to connect the left and right edges. And since I just made the rules up, there are zero existing opening books or strategy guides to crib from.

First, Ultimate-style move routing cuts the branching factor. Wherever you play sets a row-and-column crosshair where your opponent must play next (unless both lines are full, which gives them a wildcard anywhere on the board). Instead of searching all 49 squares, MCTS only has to search 8 to 12 legal moves per turn.

Second, pinch flips make draws impossible on a square grid. Normally, a connection game on a square grid can deadlock whenever Red and Blue form a 2×2 checkerboard (R B over B R), which blocks both players at once. Crosshair kills deadlocks with two flip rules when you place a stone: sandwiching an adjacent enemy stone in a line (R [B] R) flips it to your color, and completing your diagonal of a 2×2 checkerboard (R B / B R) flips both enemy stones (cascading if a flip creates another 2×2 checkerboard). Across 70,000 test games, every single game ended in a win (0 draws) in an average of 27 moves.

How the AlphaZero loop works

To define it exactly, an AlphaZero loop uses a single neural network that looks at a board position and outputs a move prior P(s,a)P(s,a)P(s,a) over the 49 squares and a win value v∈[−1,+1]v \in [-1, +1]v∈[−1,+1].

During search, MCTS runs 450 simulations per move and picks actions using the PUCT formula:

As you can see though, the favorite move slowly changes as visits build up. The magic here is that this turns MCTS into a policy improvement step, which gives us a feedback loop: the network starts with a rough prior PPP, tree search checks what happens several moves ahead to produce a better visit distribution π\piπ, and we train the policy network to match π\piπ. Meanwhile, the value network is further trained on the actual win/loss outcomes z∈{−1,+1}z \in \{-1, +1\}z∈{−1,+1} of the finished self-play games. Because Crosshair games finish in ~27 moves with zero draws, any position the value network overrates gets played into during the next generation of self-play, loses, and gets corrected.

The network itself is a 114,290-parameter residual MLP rather than a 2D ConvNet. A move at d4 projects sightlines across all of Row 4 and File D at once, so a fully connected first layer sees the whole crosshair immediately. Better yet, 114,290 float32 weights (457 KB) fit inside CPU L2 cache and evaluate a position in ~25 microseconds of flat dot products, which lets single-threaded WebAssembly run up to 40,000 MCTS simulations in 1.2 seconds.

The input is six 7×7 feature planes flattened into 294 floats: our stones, the opponent's stones, the legal moves in the active crosshair, how many enemy stones each legal move would flip (min⁡(flips,4)/4\min(\text{flips}, 4)/4min(flips,4)/4), the opponent's last move, and the turn counter (ply/49\text{ply}/49ply/49). That 294-float vector passes through a 294 → 128 linear stem with a ReLU activation, followed by two 128-wide residual blocks (128 → ReLU → 128, added back to the block's input via a skip connection x + f(x) and passed through ReLU). From there the trunk splits into two output heads: a policy head (128 → 49 linear layer with masked Softmax over legal moves) that outputs a probability P(s,a)∈[0,1]P(s,a) \in [0, 1]P(s,a)∈[0,1] for each square, and a value head (128 → 32 with ReLU, then 32 → 1 with tanh) that outputs a single win/loss score v∈[−1,+1]v \in [-1, +1]v∈[−1,+1]. Training is written in Python using raw JAX (@jax.jit and a 40-line AdamW loop on CUDA, no Flax or PyTorch), and the exported weights are compiled directly into the zero-dependency Rust engine (wasm32-unknown-unknown) for browser inference.

Training it on one GPU in three minutes

Each self-play generation takes about 40 seconds on my desktop: Rust plays 220 self-play games across 16 CPU threads (~18s), JAX trains the network for 3 epochs on the RTX 4060 Ti at 94,000 samples/second (~3s), and Rust runs 60-game arena matches (~18s). Almost 90% of the wall-clock time is CPU tree search, not GPU backprop.

Four generations (880 self-play games) took about three minutes end to end. Across those four generations, top-1 move agreement with 450-sim MCTS climbs from 19% to 34%, and value sign accuracy climbs from a 49% coin flip to 64%:

In 60-game arena matches at 400 simulations per move, the trained network crushes its untrained Gen 0 starting point (90%–97% win rate).
