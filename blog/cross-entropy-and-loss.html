Every training loop you've ever seen has a line that looks something like this:

Two lines. You call a loss function, you backpropagate the result. And if you're training a language model, criterion is almost certainly cross-entropy loss. Every GPT, every LLaMA, every BERT pretraining run uses it.

So if cross-entropy is the loss function for language modeling, what exactly is it measuring? Like, what IS entropy? Why not just compute accuracy (percentage of correct predictions) and minimize that?

I operated with a vague mental model of this for a long time. "It measures how different two distributions are." Sure. But that's hand-wavy enough to be useless when you're trying to debug a training run or figure out why your perplexity is stuck at 45.

Let me try to build the intuition from scratch.

Why not just use accuracy?

This is worth addressing upfront, because it's the obvious question. If the model predicts the next token, and we know the actual next token, why not just score it as "right or wrong" and try to maximize the percentage of correct predictions?

Two problems. First, accuracy isn't differentiable; it's a step function. The model either got it right or it didn't, and there's no gradient to flow backwards. You can't do calculus on "right or wrong."

Second, accuracy throws away information. Suppose the correct next token is "the." Model A assigns 51% probability to "the" and 49% to "a." Model B assigns 99% probability to "the." Accuracy says both models scored a perfect 1.0 on this example. But Model B clearly has a better grip on the distribution, and we need a loss function that can see the difference.

Let me just show that. The viz below flips between the two situations. Accuracy doesn't move. Cross-entropy does.

Cross-entropy solves both of these problems. It's smooth, differentiable, and it cares about the probabilities the model assigns, not just whether the top prediction was correct.

Entropy: the floor

Before we get to cross-entropy, we need entropy itself. Shannon invented it in 1948,[^1] and honestly the core idea is simpler than it sounds.

Entropy measures the irreducible uncertainty in a probability distribution. How surprised should you be, on average, when you observe an outcome?

H(p)=−∑ipilog⁡2piH(p) = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_iH(p)=−i∑​pi​log2​pi​

That's it. For each possible outcome, multiply its probability by the log of its probability, sum them up, flip the sign.

Play with the slider for a second before we do the coin-and-dice math. Drag concentration from uniform toward one-hot and watch the needle fall.

Entropy is a property of the distribution's shape, no target required. Flat distribution, needle pinned at log₂(5) ≈ 2.32. Collapse onto a single outcome and entropy goes to zero. This is the floor that cross-entropy can never beat.

Some quick examples to build intuition. A fair coin has two outcomes, each with probability 0.5:

H=−(0.5⋅log⁡20.5+0.5⋅log⁡20.5)=−(0.5⋅(−1)+0.5⋅(−1))=1 bitH = -(0.5 \cdot \log_2 0.5 + 0.5 \cdot \log_2 0.5) = -(0.5 \cdot (-1) + 0.5 \cdot (-1)) = 1 \text{ bit}H=−(0.5⋅log2​0.5+0.5⋅log2​0.5)=−(0.5⋅(−1)+0.5⋅(−1))=1 bit

One bit of entropy. You need exactly one bit of information to describe the outcome of a fair coin flip. That checks out.

A fair six-sided die:

H=−6⋅(16⋅log⁡216)≈2.585 bitsH = -6 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{6} \cdot \log_2 \frac{1}{6}\right) \approx 2.585 \text{ bits}H=−6⋅(61​⋅log2​61​)≈2.585 bits

More outcomes means more uncertainty means more entropy. A loaded die that lands on 6 about 90% of the time has much lower entropy. You're rarely surprised.

The key idea: entropy is low when the distribution is peaked (one outcome dominates), and high when the distribution is spread out (many outcomes are likely). A perfectly uniform distribution over nnn outcomes has entropy log⁡2n\log_2 nlog2​n, which is the maximum possible.

Why does this matter for language? Because language has structure. The next word after "the United" is almost certainly "States" or "Kingdom" or "Nations," and the entropy of the true next-token distribution in that context is low. After "I want to", the entropy is much higher because there are lots of reasonable continuations. A good language model should internalize this: be confident when the context demands it, uncertain when it doesn't.

Cross-entropy: how surprised is the model?

Now we get to the thing we actually compute during training. Cross-entropy measures something slightly different from entropy. Entropy asks "how uncertain is the true distribution?" Cross-entropy asks "how surprised is model qqq when reality follows distribution ppp?"

H(p,q)=−∑ipilog⁡2qiH(p, q) = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 q_iH(p,q)=−i∑​pi​log2​qi​

Notice the subtle difference from regular entropy. We still weight by the true probabilities pip_ipi​ (because reality determines which outcomes happen), but we take the log of the model's probabilities qiq_iqi​ (because the model's predictions determine how surprised it is).

If the model perfectly matches reality (q=pq = pq=p), then cross-entropy equals entropy: H(p,q)=H(p)H(p, q) = H(p)H(p,q)=H(p). That's the best you can do. You can't be less surprised than the inherent uncertainty of the data.

If the model is wrong (q≠pq \neq pq=p), cross-entropy is always higher than entropy. The model is more surprised than it needs to be, and that extra surprise is what we're trying to eliminate during training.

Let me make this concrete. Say the true distribution over the next word is p=[0.7,0.2,0.1]p = [0.7, 0.2, 0.1]p=[0.7,0.2,0.1] for tokens ["the", "a", "one"]. And our model predicts q=[0.5,0.3,0.2]q = [0.5, 0.3, 0.2]q=[0.5,0.3,0.2].

True entropy:

H(p)=−(0.7⋅log⁡20.7+0.2⋅log⁡20.2+0.1⋅log⁡20.1)≈1.157 bitsH(p) = -(0.7 \cdot \log_2 0.7 + 0.2 \cdot \log_2 0.2 + 0.1 \cdot \log_2 0.1) \approx 1.157 \text{ bits}H(p)=−(0.7⋅log2​0.7+0.2⋅log2​0.2+0.1⋅log2​0.1)≈1.157 bits

Cross-entropy:

H(p,q)=−(0.7⋅log⁡20.5+0.2⋅log⁡20.3+0.1⋅log⁡20.2)≈1.280 bitsH(p, q) = -(0.7 \cdot \log_2 0.5 + 0.2 \cdot \log_2 0.3 + 0.1 \cdot \log_2 0.2) \approx 1.280 \text{ bits}H(p,q)=−(0.7⋅log2​0.5+0.2⋅log2​0.3+0.1⋅log2​0.2)≈1.280 bits

The model is paying an extra ~0.12 bits of surprise because its distribution doesn't match reality. It's assigning too little probability to "the" and too much to the rarer words.

KL divergence: the gap

KL divergence is going to show up constantly in the ML literature, especially once we get to RLHF and alignment, so it deserves its own section.

KL divergence is just the difference between cross-entropy and entropy:

DKL(p∥q)=H(p,q)−H(p)D_{\text{KL}}(p \| q) = H(p, q) - H(p)DKL​(p∥q)=H(p,q)−H(p)

Or, equivalently:

DKL(p∥q)=∑ipilog⁡2piqiD_{\text{KL}}(p \| q) = \sum_i p_i \log_2 \frac{p_i}{q_i}DKL​(p∥q)=i∑​pi​log2​qi​pi​​

It answers a very specific question: how much extra surprise does model qqq cause, beyond the irreducible uncertainty in ppp?

KL divergence has a beautiful property. It's always ≥0\geq 0≥0, and it equals zero if and only if p=qp = qp=q. This is called Gibbs' inequality, and it's what makes cross-entropy work as a loss function. The minimum of the loss surface is exactly where the model distribution matches the true distribution.

When you minimize cross-entropy, you're also minimizing KL divergence (because H(p)H(p)H(p) is a constant that doesn't depend on the model). So minimizing cross-entropy, minimizing KL divergence, and maximizing log-likelihood are all the same optimization objective, just written in different ways.[^2]

One important gotcha: KL divergence is not symmetric. DKL(p∥q)≠DKL(q∥p)D_{\text{KL}}(p \| q) \neq D_{\text{KL}}(q \| p)DKL​(p∥q)=DKL​(q∥p) in general. This means it's not a "distance" in the mathematical sense. The direction matters. DKL(p∥q)D_{\text{KL}}(p \| q)DKL​(p∥q) asks "how well does qqq approximate ppp?" while DKL(q∥p)D_{\text{KL}}(q \| p)DKL​(q∥p) asks the reverse. Language model training uses the first form: how well does our model approximate reality.

The asymmetry isn't a math curiosity. It changes what kind of model you end up with. Watch the same qqq get scored both ways:

Reality is bimodal (peaks at b and f). When q covers both modes, forward KL is small but reverse KL is bigger. When q collapses onto one mode, reverse KL is small but forward KL explodes. Pretraining minimizes forward KL, so models trained on next-token data are pushed to spread mass over every plausible continuation rather than commit to one.

Perplexity: making it interpretable

Cross-entropy in bits is hard to intuit. Is 3.2 bits good? Bad? Depends on the vocabulary size and the task. Perplexity converts cross-entropy into something more tangible:

Perplexity=2H(p,q)\text{Perplexity} = 2^{H(p, q)}Perplexity=2H(p,q)

Perplexity is the effective number of choices the model is deciding between.[^3] If a model has a perplexity of 10 on English text, it's as uncertain, on average, as if it were uniformly choosing between 10 equally likely next tokens at each position.

Slide cross-entropy around and watch perplexity track 2^H. A coin flip is 1 bit. Six-sided die is ~2.6 bits. GPT-2 on WikiText-103 was ~4 bits; frontier 2026 models sit near 3 bits.

A perfect model that fully captured English text statistics might have a perplexity in the low single digits for many contexts. GPT-2 (1.5B) reported a perplexity of about 17.5 on WikiText-103 zero-shot. GPT-3 dropped that further. Modern frontier models can get below 10 on standard benchmarks. Lower perplexity means the model is less confused about what comes next.

Keep one thing in mind, though: perplexity is an average over positions. Some positions are easy (the word "States" after "United") and some are genuinely hard (the first word of a new paragraph). A model with perplexity 10 isn't wrong 90% of the time; it means that on average the model's probability distribution is as wide as a uniform distribution over 10 options. At easy positions it might be choosing between 2 options, at hard positions maybe 50.

What actually happens during training

Let's zoom into what happens at a single training step, because this is where cross-entropy goes from theory to practice.

The model receives a sequence of tokens as context. For each position, it outputs a vector of logits, one number per vocabulary token. Those logits get turned into a probability distribution via softmax. That gives us qqq, the model's predicted distribution over the next token.

Raw logits on the left, probabilities after softmax on the right. If the correct token is 'the' (index 0), the loss for this prediction is −log q('the') = −log(0.603) ≈ 0.51 nats. That's the only number cross-entropy cares about — the probability the model assigned to the token that came next.

The true distribution ppp is a one-hot vector. The actual next token in the training data has probability 1, everything else has probability 0. This is a crucial simplification. With a one-hot target, the cross-entropy formula collapses:

H(p,q)=−∑ipilog⁡qi=−1⋅log⁡qcorrect−∑i≠correct0⋅log⁡qi=−log⁡qcorrectH(p, q) = -\sum_i p_i \log q_i = -1 \cdot \log q_{\text{correct}} - \sum_{i \neq \text{correct}} 0 \cdot \log q_i = -\log q_{\text{correct}}H(p,q)=−i∑​pi​logqi​=−1⋅logqcorrect​−i=correct∑​0⋅logqi​=−logqcorrect​

That's it. Cross-entropy with a one-hot target is just the negative log probability of the correct token. −log⁡qcorrect-\log q_{\text{correct}}−logqcorrect​.

If the model assigns probability 0.9 to the correct token, the loss is −log⁡2(0.9)≈0.15-\log_2(0.9) \approx 0.15−log2​(0.9)≈0.15 bits. Small loss, the model is confident and correct. If it assigns probability 0.01 to the correct token, the loss is −log⁡2(0.01)≈6.64-\log_2(0.01) \approx 6.64−log2​(0.01)≈6.64 bits. Huge loss. The model was surprised by what actually happened.

Notice the shape of that curve. If the model assigns probability 1.0 to the correct token, loss is zero. Perfect. If it assigns probability approaching 0, loss goes to infinity. The log function provides this natural asymmetry for free.

Implementation: it's four lines

In PyTorch, cross-entropy loss takes raw logits (pre-softmax), not probabilities. It applies softmax internally for numerical stability. The log-sum-exp trick avoids the overflow and underflow that can happen if you softmax first and take the log separately.

That's the entire loss computation for a language model. In a real training loop the logits tensor has shape (batch_size * sequence_length, vocab_size), and the target tensor has shape (batch_size * sequence_length), so you're computing this loss across every position in every sequence in the batch, then averaging.

Misconceptions

"Lower loss means better generation quality." Cross-entropy measures how well the model's distribution matches the training data distribution. A model with perfect cross-entropy would assign exactly the right probability to every token in every context. But generation involves sampling from that distribution, and distribution matching doesn't guarantee that samples will be coherent, interesting, or useful. A model can have great loss numbers and still generate repetitive, boring text. This is part of why the field moved toward RLHF and DPO for alignment, which we'll cover in Arc 7. Loss measures distribution fit. Quality is something else.

"KL divergence is a distance metric." It's not. A distance metric has to be symmetric, so the distance from A to B equals the distance from B to A. KL divergence doesn't satisfy that. DKL(p∥q)D_{\text{KL}}(p \| q)DKL​(p∥q) can be wildly different from DKL(q∥p)D_{\text{KL}}(q \| p)DKL​(q∥p), and this isn't just a technicality. The two directions have different practical behavior. Forward KL (DKL(p∥q)D_{\text{KL}}(p \| q)DKL​(p∥q), what we use in pretraining) tends to produce models that are mean-seeking: they try to cover all the modes of the true distribution. Reverse KL tends to produce models that are mode-seeking: they collapse to a single mode. This distinction becomes critical when we get to variational inference and policy optimization.[^4]

"Perplexity of 10 means the model is wrong 90% of the time." No. Perplexity is about the width of the model's probability distribution, not its accuracy. A perplexity of 10 means the model's average uncertainty is equivalent to choosing uniformly from 10 options. It might be getting the top-1 prediction correct 60% of the time while spreading the remaining probability mass across 9 alternatives. Perplexity and accuracy are correlated but not interchangeable.

"Cross-entropy values are comparable across models." Only if the tokenizers are the same. A model that tokenizes text into longer pieces will naturally have higher per-token loss than one that splits into shorter pieces, because each token is carrying more information. When you see a perplexity number for a model, the tokenizer is part of the number. Comparing perplexities across models with different vocabularies is apples and oranges unless someone normalizes by bits-per-byte or bits-per-character.

These ideas in the wild

Cross-entropy is still the workhorse of pretraining. Every major foundation model still uses it. But the relationship between loss and downstream capability has gotten more complicated, not simpler.

Scaling laws. Kaplan et al.[^5] and Hoffmann et al.[^6] (the Chinchilla paper) showed that pretraining loss follows predictable power laws as you scale compute, data, and parameters. That predictability is why labs can spend hundreds of millions on a training run and have a pretty good idea of what final loss they'll hit before they even start. What scaling laws don't predict cleanly is downstream task performance: a small drop in pretraining loss might unlock a capability that didn't exist before, or it might not matter at all.

DPO and preference losses. Cross-entropy trains the model to predict the next token in human-written text, but what we actually want is a model that generates helpful, harmless, honest responses. RLHF adds a reward model on top of cross-entropy pretraining. DPO (direct preference optimization)[^7] goes further and reformulates the reward model objective as a modified cross-entropy-style loss over preference pairs. The machinery is the same. Take the log probability of a sequence, compare it to a reference model's log probability, and optimize the gap. The 2024-2026 alignment stack is almost entirely built out of cross-entropy variants.

Per-token loss as interpretability. Researchers have started using per-token loss breakdowns as a window into what models have and haven't learned. Tokens with persistently high loss across training are often genuinely ambiguous (multiple valid continuations), while tokens with loss that spikes on specific examples can reveal distribution shift or data quality issues. Loss has become a diagnostic tool in its own right, not just a number to minimize.

What's next

Cross-entropy is a simple idea with a lot of baggage. Take the true distribution, take the model's distribution, ask how surprised the model is by reality, minimize that surprise. The model predicts a distribution, reality reveals an answer, cross-entropy measures the gap, and gradients close it.
