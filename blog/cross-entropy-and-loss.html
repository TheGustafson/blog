A PyTorch training step often includes these two lines:

Cross-entropy is a standard next-token training loss. But what does it measure, and why use it instead of accuracy?

Accuracy counts how often the most likely token matches the observation. Cross-entropy also measures the probability assigned to that observation.

Why not just use accuracy?

If the model predicts the next token, and we know the actual next token, why not just score it as "right or wrong" and try to maximize the percentage of correct predictions?

Two problems. First, top-1 accuracy is constant while small logit changes leave the winning class unchanged, then jumps when the winner changes. Its gradient is zero almost everywhere and undefined at those boundaries, so it gives gradient descent no useful local direction.

Second, accuracy throws away information. Suppose the correct next token is "the." Model A assigns 51% probability to "the" and 49% to "a." Model B assigns 99% probability to "the." Accuracy says both models scored a perfect 1.0 on this example. Model B assigns higher probability to this observed token, so it gets lower log loss on this example.

Cross-entropy solves both of these problems. It's smooth, differentiable, and it cares about the probabilities the model assigns, not just whether the top prediction was correct.

Entropy: the floor

Before we get to cross-entropy, we need entropy itself. Shannon introduced it in his 1948 work on information theory.

Entropy measures the irreducible uncertainty in a probability distribution. How surprised should you be, on average, when you observe an outcome?

H(p)=−∑ipilog⁡2piH(p) = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_iH(p)=−i∑​pi​log2​pi​

For each possible outcome, multiply its probability by the log of its probability, sum them up, flip the sign.

The slider varies the distribution's concentration. Drag concentration from uniform toward one-hot and watch the needle fall.

Entropy is a property of the distribution's shape, no target required. Flat distribution, needle pinned at log₂(5) ≈ 2.32. Collapse onto a single outcome and entropy goes to zero. This is the floor that, in expectation under p, cross-entropy can't go below.

A fair coin has two outcomes, each with probability 0.5:

H=−(0.5⋅log⁡20.5+0.5⋅log⁡20.5)=−(0.5⋅(−1)+0.5⋅(−1))=1 bitH = -(0.5 \cdot \log_2 0.5 + 0.5 \cdot \log_2 0.5) = -(0.5 \cdot (-1) + 0.5 \cdot (-1)) = 1 \text{ bit}H=−(0.5⋅log2​0.5+0.5⋅log2​0.5)=−(0.5⋅(−1)+0.5⋅(−1))=1 bit

One bit of entropy. You need exactly one bit of information to describe the outcome of a fair coin flip.

A fair six-sided die:

H=−6⋅(16⋅log⁡216)≈2.585 bitsH = -6 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{6} \cdot \log_2 \frac{1}{6}\right) \approx 2.585 \text{ bits}H=−6⋅(61​⋅log2​61​)≈2.585 bits

A uniform distribution has more entropy when it has more outcomes. A loaded die that lands on 6 about 90% of the time has much lower entropy. You're rarely surprised.

Entropy is low when the distribution is peaked (one outcome dominates), and high when the distribution is spread out (many outcomes are likely). A perfectly uniform distribution over nnn outcomes has entropy log⁡2n\log_2 nlog2​n, which is the maximum possible.

Why does this matter for language? Because language has structure. The next word after "the United" is almost certainly "States" or "Kingdom" or "Nations," and the entropy of the true next-token distribution in that context is low. After "I want to", the entropy is much higher because there are lots of reasonable continuations. A good language model should internalize this: be confident when the context demands it, uncertain when it doesn't.

Cross-entropy: how surprised is the model?

Entropy averages surprise using the distribution's own probabilities. Cross-entropy averages the surprise assigned by model qqq when outcomes follow distribution ppp:

H(p,q)=−∑ipilog⁡2qiH(p, q) = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 q_iH(p,q)=−i∑​pi​log2​qi​

The expectation uses pip_ipi​, the probabilities governing which outcomes occur, while the logarithm uses the model probabilities qiq_iqi​.

When q=pq = pq=p, cross-entropy reaches its minimum, H(p,q)=H(p)H(p, q) = H(p)H(p,q)=H(p). The uncertainty in ppp remains even with a matching model.

When q≠pq \neq pq=p, cross-entropy is higher. Minimizing it encourages the model distribution to match ppp.

Suppose the true distribution over the next word is p=[0.7,0.2,0.1]p = [0.7, 0.2, 0.1]p=[0.7,0.2,0.1] for tokens ["the", "a", "one"]. And our model predicts q=[0.5,0.3,0.2]q = [0.5, 0.3, 0.2]q=[0.5,0.3,0.2].

True entropy:

H(p)=−(0.7⋅log⁡20.7+0.2⋅log⁡20.2+0.1⋅log⁡20.1)≈1.157 bitsH(p) = -(0.7 \cdot \log_2 0.7 + 0.2 \cdot \log_2 0.2 + 0.1 \cdot \log_2 0.1) \approx 1.157 \text{ bits}H(p)=−(0.7⋅log2​0.7+0.2⋅log2​0.2+0.1⋅log2​0.1)≈1.157 bits

Cross-entropy:

H(p,q)=−(0.7⋅log⁡20.5+0.2⋅log⁡20.3+0.1⋅log⁡20.2)≈1.280 bitsH(p, q) = -(0.7 \cdot \log_2 0.5 + 0.2 \cdot \log_2 0.3 + 0.1 \cdot \log_2 0.2) \approx 1.280 \text{ bits}H(p,q)=−(0.7⋅log2​0.5+0.2⋅log2​0.3+0.1⋅log2​0.2)≈1.280 bits

The model is paying an extra ~0.12 bits of surprise because its distribution doesn't match reality. It's assigning too little probability to "the" and too much to the rarer words.

KL divergence: the gap

KL divergence is just the difference between cross-entropy and entropy:

DKL(p∥q)=H(p,q)−H(p)D_{\text{KL}}(p \| q) = H(p, q) - H(p)DKL​(p∥q)=H(p,q)−H(p)

Or, equivalently:

DKL(p∥q)=∑ipilog⁡2piqiD_{\text{KL}}(p \| q) = \sum_i p_i \log_2 \frac{p_i}{q_i}DKL​(p∥q)=i∑​pi​log2​qi​pi​​

KL divergence is always ≥0\geq 0≥0, and it equals zero if and only if p=qp = qp=q. This is called Gibbs' inequality, and it's what makes cross-entropy work as a loss function. Over unrestricted probability distributions, the minimum is at q=pq=pq=p. A finite model family may not be able to represent that distribution.

When you minimize cross-entropy, you're also minimizing KL divergence (because H(p)H(p)H(p) is a constant that doesn't depend on the model). So minimizing cross-entropy, minimizing KL divergence, and maximizing log-likelihood are all the same optimization objective, just written in different ways.

KL divergence is not symmetric. DKL(p∥q)≠DKL(q∥p)D_{\text{KL}}(p \| q) \neq D_{\text{KL}}(q \| p)DKL​(p∥q)=DKL​(q∥p) in general. This means it's not a "distance" in the mathematical sense. The direction matters. DKL(p∥q)D_{\text{KL}}(p \| q)DKL​(p∥q) asks "how well does qqq approximate ppp?" while DKL(q∥p)D_{\text{KL}}(q \| p)DKL​(q∥p) asks the reverse. Language model training uses the first form: how well does our model approximate reality.

Perplexity: making it interpretable

Cross-entropy in bits is hard to intuit. Is 3.2 bits good? Bad? Depends on the vocabulary size and the task. Perplexity converts cross-entropy into something more tangible:

Perplexity=2H(p,q)\text{Perplexity} = 2^{H(p, q)}Perplexity=2H(p,q)

That formula assumes cross-entropy is in bits (log base 2). If your loss is reported in nats (natural log), as PyTorch's CrossEntropyLoss does, the equivalent is Perplexity=eloss\text{Perplexity} = e^{\text{loss}}Perplexity=eloss. Either way, the answer is the same number; it's just a base change.

Perplexity is the number of equally likely choices that would produce the same average log loss on the observed tokens. A perplexity of 10 corresponds to a geometric-mean probability of 0.1 for those tokens. It does not measure how many tokens the model actually considers plausible.

Perplexity comparisons need the same evaluation text, tokenization, context policy, and scoring procedure. A lower value means better log loss under that evaluation; it does not establish broader model quality.

A sharp distribution can still have high perplexity if it confidently predicts the wrong tokens. Conversely, a model can have low average loss while making occasional costly mistakes. Perplexity averages those observed losses; it is neither error rate nor distribution width.

What actually happens during training

The model receives a sequence of tokens as context. For each position, it outputs a vector of logits, one number per vocabulary token. Those logits get turned into a probability distribution via softmax. That gives us qqq, the model's predicted distribution over the next token.

The dataset supplies one observed next token per position, not the full conditional distribution ppp. We encode that observation as a one-hot target. Averaging these sampled losses estimates cross-entropy under the data distribution; the underlying distribution can still assign probability to many continuations.

When softmax and cross-entropy are combined, the gradient on each logit simplifies to:

∂L∂zj=qj−yj\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j} = q_j - y_j∂zj​∂L​=qj​−yj​

For loss in nats, this is predicted probability minus target. Loss in bits adds a factor of 1/ln⁡21/\ln 21/ln2. For the softmax example above, q=[0.603,0.222,0.135,0.030,0.011]q = [0.603, 0.222, 0.135, 0.030, 0.011]q=[0.603,0.222,0.135,0.030,0.011] with target class 0:

The gradient on the correct logit is negative (so the optimizer pushes that logit up); the gradient on every wrong logit is positive (so those logits get pushed down), in proportion to how much probability mass they were wrongly carrying. The exponential, the log, and the sum all cancel exactly when softmax and cross-entropy are paired.

Implementation

In PyTorch, cross-entropy loss takes raw logits (pre-softmax), not probabilities. It computes log probabilities internally in a numerically stable way. The log-sum-exp trick avoids the overflow and underflow that can happen if you softmax first and take the log separately.

In a real training loop the logits tensor has shape (batch_size * sequence_length, vocab_size), and the target tensor has shape (batch_size * sequence_length), so you're computing this loss across every position in every sequence in the batch, then averaging.

Common variants

Label smoothing. Instead of a strict one-hot target, mix in a small amount of uniform mass: target = (1−ϵ) one-hot+ϵ/V(1-\epsilon)\,\text{one-hot} + \epsilon/V(1−ϵ)one-hot+ϵ/V. The loss now penalizes the model for being too confident on the training token, which acts as a regularizer. Used in Inception-v3, and supported in PyTorch via CrossEntropyLoss(label_smoothing=...).

Class weighting. When some target classes are much rarer than others, you can pass per-class weights so the loss doesn't get dominated by the common classes. PyTorch supports this via the weight argument to CrossEntropyLoss.

Focal loss. Reshapes cross-entropy by multiplying it with (1−qcorrect)γ(1 - q_{\text{correct}})^\gamma(1−qcorrect​)γ, which down-weights examples the model is already getting confidently right and concentrates learning on the hard ones. Originally motivated by extreme class imbalance in dense object detection.

Binary / multi-label cross-entropy. When labels are not mutually exclusive (a tag predictor, a multi-label classifier), you don't want softmax across all labels. You want a sigmoid per label and binary cross-entropy on each. PyTorch's BCEWithLogitsLoss is the numerically stable version.

For language modeling specifically, plain softmax cross-entropy with one-hot targets is still the dominant pretraining objective. The variants above mostly come up in classifier heads, fine-tuning recipes, and adjacent tasks.

Misconceptions

"Lower loss means better generation quality." Log loss is strictly proper: its expected value is minimized by reporting the true distribution. That does not guarantee calibration for a finite, imperfectly trained model, or helpful behavior after decoding. Loss evaluates prediction probabilities; generation quality also depends on the task and decoding policy.

"KL divergence is a distance metric." It is not symmetric and does not satisfy the triangle inequality. The two directions weight errors differently. Descriptions such as forward KL being mode-covering and reverse KL being mode-seeking refer to particular restricted approximation families; both have the same unrestricted optimum, q=pq=pq=p.

"Perplexity of 10 means the model is wrong 90% of the time." It means the geometric mean of the probabilities assigned to observed tokens is 0.1. Top-1 accuracy cannot be recovered from that number. A model can put nearly all its mass on the wrong token and receive very high perplexity despite its narrow distribution.

"Cross-entropy values are comparable across models." Only under a matched evaluation protocol, including text, tokenizer, context lengths, masking, and averaging. A model that tokenizes text into longer pieces can have higher per-token loss than one that splits into shorter pieces, because each token is carrying more information. When you see a perplexity number for a model, the tokenizer is part of the number. Across tokenizers, report a common unit such as bits per byte or bits per character on matched text.
