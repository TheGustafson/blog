Crosshair-Bench: Evaluating Frontier Models on an Unseen Game

October 28, 20264 min read

If you ask a language model to play Chess, Othello, or Connect Four, it is hard to tell how much of its play comes from calculating on the spot and how much comes from having read millions of games and strategy guides during training.

On October 25, 2026, I invented a 7×7 connection-and-flip board game called Crosshair and trained a 114,290-parameter (457 KB) AlphaZero network for it on one desktop GPU in six minutes. Because the game is brand new, none of the models in this tournament have ever seen a position or read a word of advice about it. Should be safe for a few generations yet!

This note is the companion writeup to the Crosshair LLM Benchmark, where you can click into any model or engine to inspect its stats and step through every game it played.

How the tournament works

I tested 20 OpenRouter model configurations (5 labs × Flagship & Flash tiers × 2 thinking levels) alongside four search rungs from ai-crosshair (random-uct-50, az-puct-1, az-puct-400, and az-puct-4000) across 480 tournament games (TheGustafson/crosshair-bench).

Every LLM pairing is a 4-game match played from balanced 2-ply openings with colors swapped, so each side plays Red twice and Blue twice from the same starting boards. I ran round-robins within the Flagship and Flash tiers, matched every model against the engines, pitted each lab's high-thinking Flash model against its low-thinking Flagship, and ran a denser 16-game-per-pair round-robin among the engines to anchor the rating scale at az-puct-1 = 1200 Elo.

On each turn, the harness sends a stateless prompt with the four-sentence ruleset, the 7×7 ASCII board (a1..g7), stone coordinate lists, the active row/column crosshair constraint, and the legal moves. Behind the scenes, az-puct-400 evaluates every position before and after the chosen move as a shadow oracle to measure Top-1 move agreement and blunder rate (moves that drop at least 10% win probability).

Against the AlphaZero search ladder

Matching the 20 LLM configurations against the Monte Carlo Tree Search and AlphaZero engines (4 games per pair, 240 games total) shows where scratchpad reasoning sits relative to tree search:

  • Top reasoning models beat random-uct-50 (1135 Elo) and outscore the zero-search policy net (az-puct-1, 1200 Elo): gemini-4-argon@high (1440 Elo), gpt-6.1-sol@low (1408 Elo), and gpt-6.1-sol@high (1398 Elo) all swept random-uct-50 4–0. Nine of the twenty LLM configurations rated above az-puct-1, and eleven rated above random-uct-50. When the neural net isn't allowed to search ahead, a frontier model thinking out loud in its scratchpad can genuinely outplay it.
  • Turning on 400 MCTS simulations (az-puct-400, 1888 Elo) flips the score to 79–1: Across 80 games, 19 of the 20 LLM configurations were swept 0–4. Only gemini-4-argon@high won a game (1–3), converting a 38-ply Blue win after az-puct-400 overpushed at f7 on ply 32.
  • At 4,000 simulations (az-puct-4000, 2343 Elo), the 114K network went 80–0: Not a single LLM won a game. Even when gpt-6.1-sol@high spent 70 seconds and 3,060 reasoning tokens per move ($0.49 per game), az-puct-4000 (0.28 seconds per move, $0.00) locked the board by ply 16. Fifty cents and a full minute of prose per move is still no match for a quarter-second of real tree search.

Thinking budget beats model size (16–4)

To test how much playing strength comes from base model size versus test-time thinking budget, I matched each model's @high and @low/@none settings (@high won 25–15 and gained +149 Elo on average), and then pitted each lab's smaller Flash @high model directly against its own Flagship @low/@none model.

The smaller Flash models with @high thinking beat their own labs' low-effort Flagships 16–4 (80%), usually at a fraction of the cost:

Not a single low-effort Flagship won its mini-match against its own lab's smaller Flash sibling. claude-haiku-5.5@high swept claude-opus-5.5@low 4–0 at 16× lower cost per game, and qwen3.8-flash@high swept qwen3.8-max@none 4–0 at 13× lower cost. When you cannot coast on memorized opening patterns, having tokens to actually trace moves on the board matters much more than parameter count.

The Flagship and Flash round-robins

In the two 5×5 round-robins (4 games per pair, 40 games each) comparing the five labs head to head:

  • Flagship @high: gpt-6.1-sol@high (12–4) finished first in the round-robin by sweeping claude-opus-5.5@high (4–0) and qwen3.8-max@high (4–0) while splitting 2–2 with gemini-4-argon@high and deepseek-v4-pro@high. gemini-4-argon@high (11–5) finished one game back in the round-robin (and #1 overall on the Elo leaderboard thanks to its win over az-puct-400), while claude-opus-5.5@high (9–7) beat Argon 3–1.
  • Flash @high: gemini-3.8-flash@high (13–3, 1328 Elo) ran away with the Flash bracket and recorded the highest az-puct-400 Top-1 move agreement of any model in the tournament (46.3% across 512 turns), finishing ahead of three of the five Flagship @high models overall. (Apparently Flash did not get the memo that it was supposed to stay in the budget bracket.)
